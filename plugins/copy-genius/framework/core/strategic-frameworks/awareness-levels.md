## Core principle

A market does not exist as a single uniform audience. Within any market, prospects sit at DIFFERENT distances from the buying decision — based on how much they know about their own desire, the available solutions, your product category, and your specific brand.

This distance is **awareness**. There are five distinct levels, each demanding a fundamentally different approach.

**The psychological wall principle**: each level is separated from the next by a psychological wall. A message that works at one level FAILS at adjacent levels — not works less well, but actively fails. A simple price-and-availability ad converts the Most Aware crowd; the same ad shown to a Problem Aware audience produces nothing. A long story-driven advertorial captivates the Unaware; the same content bores the Most Aware out of the room.

The copywriter's first job, before writing one word, is to determine where the dominant majority of the market sits — then craft messaging that meets them at that exact point. Every other strategic decision (headline approach, asset type, length, chain of beliefs to install, presence or absence of a Big Idea) follows from this mapping.

This framework is part of the public copywriting canon (Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising*).

## Strategic profile per level (at a glance)

Before drilling into each level's detail, three orientation points anchor the Strategist's choices: the dominant focus the page must align to, the role of the lead, and the minimum "rings" of the chain of beliefs that must be installed for the prospect to act.

| Level | Main focus | Lead role | Min rings | Ring → belief range |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Most Aware | Irresistible offer + brand positioning | N/A — the offer headline IS the message | 1 | Brand beliefs only (B1-B6 — beliefs 1-26 already held) |
| Product Aware | Unique Mechanism + brand trust | Amplify the promise — competition destruction belongs in a later, dedicated section of the page, not in the lead | 3 | Beliefs 17-26 |
| Solution Aware | Solution Mechanism (no brand — pure direct response) | Amplify the desire + make complete realization credible | 4 | Beliefs 9-17 |
| Problem Aware | Problem Mechanism (Original Problem or New Better Solution route) | Install the "1% of missing information" → external blame, not the prospect's fault | 4 | Beliefs 1-8 (+ 9-17 to bridge to the solution) |
| Unaware | The prospect's own mental state through identification (NOT product / desire / problem) | Pure identification — reflect their emotional state via story, image, or universal moment | Full chain | Full chain from belief 1 onward |

### Minimum rings of the chain of beliefs (per level)

The "rings" are abstract belief categories — strategic-level convictions the prospect must hold to act. Each ring is installed by one or more specific beliefs from the chain library (see `chain-of-beliefs.md`). These rings are STRATEGIC; the specific beliefs that fill them are tactical.

**Most Aware (1 ring)**

- "This is the best offer I'll see for this product — procrastinating could harm me"

**Product Aware (3 rings)**

- "The product has a Unique Mechanism useful for my situation"
- "The product is superior to competitors for my specific case"
- "Procrastinating could harm me"

**Solution Aware (4 rings)**

- "Your product category can satisfy my desire" (aligned with what the ads said)
- "Your category is superior to other categories solving the same desire" (Solution Mechanism + competition destruction at the category level)
- "Within the superior category, your product has a Useful Mechanism for my specific situation" (SKIP if leader-of-category exception)
- "Procrastinating could harm me"

**Problem Aware (4 rings)**

- "The brand that wrote this truly understands my problem" (aligned with what the ads said)
- "The real cause of my problem is what the problem mechanism describes" (Original Problem route: install a NEW root-cause mechanism / New Better Solution route: confirm the prospect's existing understanding of the cause)
- "There exists a product with a Useful Mechanism for that problem"
- "Procrastinating could harm me"

**Unaware (full chain)**

- The narrative installs every belief in sequence: problem exists → problem is priority → problem is worsening → previous understanding was incomplete → solution exists → solution differs from past failures → product has a unique mechanism → "I deserve this and can afford it"
- The piece traverses ALL the prior awareness levels' rings in order, layered into a narrative the prospect reads for its own sake

### How to read the table

- **Main focus** tells the Strategist what the entire piece must align to. Misalignment here breaks the funnel regardless of execution quality.
- **Lead role** tells the Strategist what the lead must DO emotionally and informationally (not how to write it — that's the specialist's job).
- **Min rings** is the minimum count of belief categories the piece must install. The piece can install MORE rings (especially if compensating for a thin ad), but never fewer.

## Diagnostic — education vs amplification

Sections discussing the problem, the desire, or the solution can appear at every awareness level. What changes is their FUNCTION. Misreading this is the most common cause of awareness misdiagnosis — especially when reading an existing piece (competitor analysis, swipe analysis, audit of a brand's current funnel).

**Educating** (Problem Aware / Unaware):

- The piece teaches the existence of the problem (or desire, for Unaware) as if new to the reader
- It shifts the reader from "I'm fine / I have no need" to "I have a problem / I have a need"
- The problem/desire section comes BEFORE any product mention
- No CTA above the fold
- Product not named in the headline
- The section is proportionally large in the piece

**Amplifying** (Most Aware / Product Aware / Solution Aware):

- The piece assumes the reader already knows the problem/desire
- It shifts the reader from "I know I have this" to "I need to act now"
- The problem/desire section is SUPPORT for the offer or the mechanism, not its precondition
- CTA may be above the fold (Most Aware, Product Aware)
- Product may be named in the headline (Most Aware, Product Aware)
- The section is proportionally small

**Diagnostic question.**

Read only the headline + the first 200 words of the piece.

> *Does the reader need this content to RECOGNIZE the problem (education)? Or does the reader use this content to FEEL the urgency of what they already know (amplification)?*

If the reader could skip the problem/desire section and still understand what's being sold → amplification → Product Aware or higher.

If skipping makes the pitch incomprehensible → education → Problem Aware or lower.

The trap this test prevents: confusing content presence with content function. A Product Aware landing can mention pain in vivid terms — those mentions amplify urgency, they do not install awareness. Calling that piece "Problem Aware with elements of Solution Aware" is the misdiagnosis. The structural signals (headline contents, CTA position, product name presence, section proportions) are the reliable diagnostic — not the topical content.

## The 5 levels of awareness

### Level 1 — Most Aware

**Definition.** The prospect knows your product, knows what it does, has been convinced it works, has decided they want it. They simply haven't completed the purchase yet — usually due to inertia, price hesitation, or distraction.

**Diagnostic signals.**

- Branded searches ("[brand name] price", "[brand name] discount", "buy [brand name]")
- Cart abandonment data showing intent without completion
- Direct questions: "where can I buy?", "is it on sale?", "do you ship to my country?"
- Returning visitors comparing prices across resellers
- Repeat customers
- Email subscribers who have opened multiple campaigns without buying

**Strategic approach.**

Direct sales. The work is removing friction, not persuading. The message contains:

- Product name (prominent)
- Price (or price comparison vs. previous)
- Reason to act NOW (limited stock, deadline, bonus, free shipping)
- Trust elements (guarantee, social proof tag)

**What the copy MUST NOT do.**

- Educate about the category, the problem, or the mechanism
- Introduce a Big Idea or hidden angle
- Tell stories
- Write long
- Sell — they're already sold

Every paragraph beyond the offer is wasted attention.

**Asset implications.**

- Format: short sales page, abandoned-cart email, retargeting ad, branded promo ad, simple landing
- Length: minimal (under 200 words is often enough)
- Education depth: zero
- Emotional setup: not needed

**Operational page blueprint.**

The shortest of all five blueprints. The offer IS the page. No mechanism explanation, no category education, no problem amplification. The buyer is ready; the page removes friction.

| # | Functional block | What it installs | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Above the fold | The single ring: "This is the best offer for this product, procrastinating could harm me" | Headline contains product name + offer (or strong offer-cue). Product image or short VSL. CTA visible. |
| 2 | Offer detail | Reinforces the single ring with concrete offer components | Components, prices, reason-why (if discounted), guarantee. Brief — the buyer already knows the product. |
| 3+ | Benefit blocks (repeatable) | Refreshes benefit memory; not all benefits are top-of-mind for the buyer | Each block has its own subheadline and minimal copy. As many or as few as the offer warrants. |
| n-1 | FAQ | Closes friction objections (shipping, returns, payment, timing) | Compact. Operational, not strategic. |
| n | Footer | Standard | Compliance + trust elements. |

The blocks are functional, not fixed slots. The exact number of sections emerges from the offer's complexity. A flat-rate product with a single bonus may have 4 sections total; a stacked offer with multiple bonuses and a guarantee may have 7. The chain remains at 1 ring; only the offer-side detail varies.

**Absolute rules at Most Aware.**

| Rule | Value |
|---|---|
| CTA above the fold | YES — and repeated 2-3x through the page |
| Product named above the fold | YES — prominent |
| Price visible in first half | YES — price comparison (was/now) where applicable |
| Big Idea required | NO — would be friction |
| Length range | Short (under 200-500 words typical) |
| Mechanism explanation | NO — the buyer already accepts the mechanism |
| Problem amplification | NO — the buyer is past the problem |

**Headline DOES contain.**

- Product name
- Offer cue (discount, deadline, bonus, free shipping)

**Headline DOES NOT contain.**

- Mechanism description
- Problem reference
- Mass-desire poetry
- Anything that asks the reader to learn before they buy

**Chain of beliefs minimum required.** Brand beliefs only (the buyer already holds beliefs 1-26 of the chain). See `chain-of-beliefs.md` → "Cross-cutting brand beliefs" section.

**Typical headline examples (illustrative).**

- "[Product Name] — Was [old price], Now [new price]"
- "Last 48 hours: 30% off [Product Name]"
- "Your cart is waiting — complete your order with free shipping today"

### Level 2 — Product Aware

**Definition.** The prospect knows the product category exists, may have considered or tried similar products, has not yet been sold on yours.

**D2C interpretation (important)**: in direct-to-consumer contexts, "Product Aware" means aware of the CATEGORY, not aware of your specific brand. The brand-aware position is reserved for Most Aware (Level 1). A prospect who knows "peptide eye creams exist" is Product Aware. A prospect who knows "Brand X's peptide eye cream exists and is decent" is Most Aware.

**Diagnostic signals.**

- Searches for the category name ("peptide eye cream", "automatic dog feeder", "language learning app")
- Comparison searches ("X vs Y vs Z", "best [category]", "[category] reviews")
- Forum threads asking "which one should I get?"
- Reading review sites
- Has tried 1+ competing products and is evaluating switching

**Strategic approach.**

Differentiation from category alternatives. The work is making YOUR product stand out from the alternatives the prospect is comparing against. Five strategic angles are available — pick ONE per piece, based on what's most underused in the category and what fits the brand's strengths:

| # | Angle | When to use | When to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | New mental image of the desire fulfilled | The desire admits vivid sensory representation; the brand has strong voice/imagery; competitors all sell by feature/spec | The market is technical and "poetic" reads as fluff |
| 2 | Sharpen an existing image of how the product satisfies a specific facet of the desire | The product has a feature competitors don't emphasize; that feature maps onto an underused sub-desire | The differentiating feature is generic or competitors are already exploiting it |
| 3 | Extend where / when / for-whom the product satisfies the desire | An untapped occasion, context, or sub-audience exists for the category; you can target it explicitly | The extension is a stretch and reads as desperation |
| 4 | Introduce new proof, details, documentation | Proof has accumulated (case studies, certifications, named-authority backing, large user base, measured results) and is genuinely fresh in market discourse | Proof is generic or matches what competitors already publish |
| 5 | Lead with the Unique Mechanism itself (the UM becomes the headline) | The UM is genuinely differentiating and codifiable; sophistication stage 3-4; competitors all sell on claim/feature, not on mechanism | No real UM exists, or the UM is too abstract to land in a headline |

Combining angles in a single headline dilutes the pitch. Pick one strong angle per piece. If multiple angles plausibly apply, the choice itself becomes part of the brief discussion with the copywriter.

**What the copy MUST NOT do.**

- Explain that the category exists (they know)
- Open with the problem (they're past it)
- Open with competition destruction (it comes after the unique mechanism section, not before)
- Treat them like Solution Aware — they don't need to be sold on the category, only on YOUR option

**Asset implications.**

- Format: comparison content, value-add landing, mid-length sales page, advertorial focused on differentiation
- Length: medium (500-1500 words)
- Education depth: differentiation depth, not category depth
- Emotional setup: light — the prospect is already engaged with the desire

**Operational page blueprint.**

The page typically composes from these functional blocks (in order). The exact section count and internal subdivisions emerge from the chain of belief required by the funnel — two Product Aware landings for the same brand can have different section structures depending on which rings beyond the 3 minimum are also installed.

| # | Functional block | What it installs | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Above the fold | Pre-positioning; signals the attack angle | Headline executes the chosen angle (1-5). Product named here. UM hinted unless angle 1. CTA above the fold is appropriate. |
| 2 | Lead | Amplifies the promise; deepens engagement with the desire the prospect already has | Does NOT attack competitors. Does NOT educate on the category. Pure amplification of why this matters now. |
| 3 | Unique Mechanism section | Ring: "The product has a Unique Mechanism useful for my situation" | Old-way / new-way framing, or new-way alone. Binds desire-fulfillment to the product. Pre-empts the "I'll go search a cheaper competitor" reflex by making the UM non-transferable to alternatives. |
| 4 | Competition destruction (by mechanism) | Ring: "The product is superior to competitors for my specific case" | Attack the mechanism the alternatives rely on, not brand names. Frame as "limitations of the [generic approach]" rather than naming competitors. Adapt to legal jurisdiction. |
| 5 | Benefits + reason-why (repeatable block) | Reinforces UM by linking each benefit to a mechanism feature | Each benefit gets a section with its own subheadline and proof. Repeats as many times as the brief's proof inventory supports. |
| 6 | Offer detail | Ring: "Procrastinating could harm me" | Components, prices, reason-why, guarantee. The buyer has been pre-sold by sections 1-5; this is where the offer becomes specific. Some offer elements (e.g., installment plans) can be teased earlier. |
| 7 | FAQ | Residual objections | Compact — 3-5 questions max. |
| 8 | Footer | Standard | Compliance + trust elements. |

The blocks are functional, not fixed slots. The exact number of sections emerges from the chain's specific ring count and the available proof inventory.

**Absolute rules at Product Aware.**

| Rule | Value |
|---|---|
| CTA above the fold | YES |
| Product named above the fold | YES |
| Price visible in first half | OPTIONAL (depends on offer construction) |
| Big Idea required | NO (optional — only if the direct approach has lost freshness) |
| Length range | Medium (500-1500 words) |
| Brand visible | YES — brand authority supports conversion at this level |

**Headline DOES contain.**

- Product name OR strong hint at the chosen attack angle (1-5)
- Hint at the Unique Mechanism (unless angle 1, which leads with sensory scene)

**Headline DOES NOT contain.**

- Problem education ("Are you tired of..." patterns)
- Category education ("What is [category]?")
- Generic claims ("The best...")

**Chain of beliefs minimum required.** Beliefs 17-26 from the chain library — "the brand has a unique advantage", "this brand fits my values", "it's now or never", "the risk is acceptable", "this proposal is superior", etc. See `chain-of-beliefs.md`.

**Typical headline examples (illustrative).**

- "Why your current [category product] is failing — and what to use instead"
- "The first [category] that [unique benefit competitors can't claim]"
- "[Specific number]% of [category] users switched after seeing this"

### Level 3 — Solution Aware

**Definition.** The prospect knows their problem and knows that solutions exist for it. They have not yet been exposed to your specific category — or if they have, they don't yet associate it with their problem.

**Diagnostic signals.**

- Generic solution searches ("how to lose weight", "how to learn a language fast", "how to fix knee pain")
- Has tried general approaches (diet, online courses, stretching) without resolution
- Reads articles about how to solve the problem
- Asks open-ended questions: "what's the best way to...?", "anyone solved this?"
- Open to new approaches; not yet committed to a particular method

**Strategic approach.**

Lead with the **unique mechanism**. The work is showing the prospect that there's a SPECIFIC way to solve their problem they haven't fully considered — and that way is yours.

The structure:
1. Open with the desire/promise (they're already sold on the desire)
2. Reveal the unique mechanism — the specific approach, method, ingredient, process, system that delivers the promise differently from generic solutions
3. Prove the mechanism works
4. Direct the desire onto your product

If the brand has no genuine unique mechanism, this is the level where the brief stalls. Solution Aware prospects in mid-sophistication markets cannot be sold on undifferentiated claims. See `unique-mechanism.md` for mechanism discovery.

**Leader-of-category exception.** When the brand leads a new category that hasn't consolidated, the category itself functions as the mechanism. The strategy shifts to selling the category.

**What the copy MUST NOT do.**

- Re-prove the problem exists (they already feel it)
- Re-prove that solutions exist (they already know)
- Skip the mechanism (the entire pivot is on it)

**Asset implications.**

- Format: advertorial, medium-length VSL (7-15 min), long sales letter, mechanism-led landing
- Length: medium-long (1500-3500 words)
- Education depth: focused on the mechanism + why it succeeds where generic solutions fail
- Emotional setup: hope/relief/curiosity (they've tried and failed before)

**Operational page blueprint.**

The page sells the CATEGORY first, then the unique mechanism within the category. The product is mentioned only after the category has been validated as the right answer to the desire. No CTA above the fold — the prospect needs to walk through the category argument before any conversion ask.

| # | Functional block | What it installs | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Above the fold | Ring: "The page was written by someone who truly understands my desire" | Headline crystallizes the desire (mass-desire-driven). Product NOT mentioned in the headline. Image supports the desire, not the product. No CTA. |
| 2 | Lead | Amplifies the desire; makes its complete realization feel credible | Adds proof elements that the desire is achievable. No competitive attack yet. |
| 3 | Solution mechanism (the category) | Ring: "Your product category is superior to other categories solving the same desire" | Sells the category as the right path. Old-way / new-way framing of categories (not products). When the brand has a real UM, this section is shorter — competitors are already educating the market on the category. When the brand is leader-of-category, this section is the longest and carries the differentiation. |
| 4 | Unique Mechanism | Ring: "Within the superior category, your product has a Useful Mechanism for my specific case" | SKIP this entire block if leader-of-category exception applies (see below). When present: bind desire-fulfillment to your product specifically. Closes with the campaign thesis ("The [qualifier] way for [avatar] to obtain [result] is through [UM]" — the canonical 4-slot formula from unique-mechanism.md §8). |
| 5 | Benefits + reason-why (repeatable) | Reinforces UM through specific benefits | Each benefit linked to a mechanism component with proof. |
| 6 | Offer detail | Ring: "Procrastinating could harm me" | Components, prices, reason-why, guarantee. The offer is descriptive (the prospect has never seen it before) — paragraphs of explanation, not just a bullet list. |
| 7 | FAQ | Residual objections | 3-5 questions max. |
| 8 | Footer | Standard | Compliance + trust elements. |

The blocks are functional, not fixed slots. Two Solution Aware landings for the same brand may have different section structures depending on chain composition.

**Leader-of-category skip rule.**

When the brand leads a category that has not consolidated, the category itself functions as the differentiator. There is no direct competition for the UM to differentiate against. The page structure changes:

- Section 3 (solution mechanism) expands and carries the whole differentiation argument
- Section 4 (Unique Mechanism) is skipped entirely
- The chain of beliefs minimum drops from 4 to 3 rings (the UM ring becomes a category ring already installed by section 3)

The Strategist surfaces this exception during brief building when the brand has no direct competitors in its category. Forcing a UM section where no real differentiation exists creates a fake mechanism that erodes trust.

**Absolute rules at Solution Aware.**

| Rule | Value |
|---|---|
| CTA above the fold | NO — first CTA only after the mechanism section(s) |
| Product named above the fold | NO — the headline is mass-desire-driven |
| Price visible in first half | NO — wait until the offer section |
| Big Idea required | YES — the prospect must connect their desire to your mechanism; the Big Idea is the bridge |
| Length range | Medium-long (1500-3500 words) |
| Brand visible above the fold | NO — pure direct response; brand emerges later if at all |

**Headline DOES contain.**

- The desire crystallized (a vivid, specific image of the end-state)
- Optionally a proof element if strong (named authority, specific number)

**Headline DOES NOT contain.**

- Product name
- The Unique Mechanism (it has to be earned through the category section)
- Generic problem statements
- CTA

**Chain of beliefs minimum required.** Beliefs 9-17 from the chain library — "the category works", "the solution addresses the root cause", "this is different from past failures", "it works for people like me", "I understand the mechanism", etc. See `chain-of-beliefs.md`.

**Typical headline examples (illustrative).**

- "The new [mechanism name] that [delivers the promise] without [common limitation]"
- "How [specific authority figure] solved [problem] with a [mechanism] no one else uses"
- "Why [generic solution] never worked — and the [mechanism] that finally does"

### Level 4 — Problem Aware

**Definition.** The prospect feels the problem — it pains them daily — but doesn't yet know what solutions exist, or doesn't believe any of them work. They may have given up actively searching and accepted the problem as permanent.

**Diagnostic signals.**

- Symptom searches ("why am I always tired", "why does my back hurt at 30", "why can't I focus")
- Complains about the problem on social media, in support groups, in conversations with friends
- Tried generic remedies (over-the-counter products, advice from friends) without success
- Frustration, resignation, or skepticism in the language they use
- May not currently be looking for a solution at all

**Strategic approach.**

Open with the PAIN. Validate it deeply. Then choose ONE of two routes — the decision shapes the entire piece:

**Route A — Original Problem.** Disagree with the prospect's current understanding of the cause. Install a NEW root-cause mechanism in their mind. Use when: their current theory of the cause is wrong, you can prove it credibly, and your product addresses the actual cause.

> Pattern: "You think [X] causes [Y]. Here's why [X] isn't the real cause. The real cause is [Z]. And [Z] is exactly what [Unique Mechanism] addresses."

Risk: higher (you're asking them to revise a belief). Payoff: higher (once installed, the new problem mechanism locks them into your solution).

**Route B — New Better Solution.** Agree with the prospect's current understanding of the problem. Offer a demonstrably superior solution than what they've tried. Use when: their theory of the cause is essentially correct, previous solutions have failed them, and your product is provably better.

> Pattern: "You already know [X] causes [Y]. You've tried [A, B, C] and they failed because [reason]. Here's what actually works: [Unique Mechanism]."

Risk: lower (you're reinforcing an existing belief). Payoff: steady (conversion depends on demonstrating superiority).

The route is a strategic choice locked at brief time. Mixing routes within a single piece creates incoherence.

**What the copy MUST NOT do.**

- Open with the offer or product
- Skip the emotional setup (they need to feel understood first)
- Mix routes mid-piece
- Promise without proof — they're skeptical from past failures

**Asset implications.**

- Format: long VSL (15+ min), long advertorial, long sales letter, story-driven landing
- Length: long (3000-10000+ words)
- Education depth: heavy — problem mechanism + solution mechanism + proof
- Emotional setup: deep validation of the pain BEFORE any pivot toward solution

**Operational page blueprint.**

The page opens on the pain, validates it deeply, then takes one of two routes — Original Problem or New Better Solution. The route choice is locked at brief time and shapes the entire piece. No CTA above the fold — the prospect needs the full emotional setup before any conversion ask.

| # | Functional block | What it installs | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Above the fold | Ring: "The brand that wrote this truly understands my problem" | Headline names the problem in the prospect's exact language. Image supports identification, not the product. No CTA. No product reference. |
| 2 | Lead — the "missing 1%" | Sets up: "Until now you haven't solved this because you were missing one piece of information — and you're about to receive it" | Externalize blame (it's not the prospect's fault). Brief — the page is earning permission to teach. |
| 3 | Problem mechanism | Ring: "The real cause of my problem is what the brand describes" | Branches by route — see table below. This is where the route decision lives. |
| 4 | Unique Mechanism | Ring: "There exists a product with a Useful Mechanism for that problem" | Built on top of the problem mechanism. The UM resolves the exact cause established in section 3. |
| 5 | Benefits + reason-why (repeatable) | Reinforces UM through specific benefits | Each benefit linked to a mechanism component with proof. Can interleave with section 4. |
| 6 | Offer detail | Ring: "Procrastinating could harm me" | Components, prices, reason-why, guarantee. Heavy descriptive — the prospect has never seen the offer. |
| 7 | FAQ | Residual objections | 3-5 questions max. |
| 8 | Footer | Standard | Compliance + trust elements. |

The blocks are functional, not fixed slots. The exact number of sections emerges from how many rings of the chain the funnel installs (4 minimum, often more in long-form pieces).

**Route decision (section 3) — Original Problem vs New Better Solution.**

The Strategist locks this at brief time. Mixing routes within a single piece creates incoherence and is a structural failure mode.

| Decision criterion | Route A — Original Problem | Route B — New Better Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Prospect's current theory of the cause | Wrong | Essentially correct |
| Brand's ability to prove a different cause | Strong (credible evidence, mechanism, authority) | Not needed |
| Brand's competitive edge | A genuinely different root-cause mechanism | A genuinely superior implementation of the agreed cause |
| Beliefs to install | One more (the prospect must abandon their existing theory before accepting the new one) | One fewer (you reinforce an existing belief instead of replacing it) |
| Risk | Higher — asking the prospect to revise a held belief | Lower — agreeing reinforces trust |
| Payoff if it lands | Higher — the new problem mechanism locks the prospect into your solution exclusively | Steady — conversion depends on demonstrated superiority |
| Pattern in section 3 | "You think [X] causes [Y]. Here's why [X] isn't the real cause. The real cause is [Z]. And [Z] is what [UM] addresses." | "You already know [X] causes [Y]. You've tried [A, B, C] and they failed because [reason]. Here's what actually works: [UM]." |
| Best for sophistication stages | 3-5 (the market has heard the obvious causes; new-cause angles still work) | 1-4 (any stage where superiority can be demonstrated) |

**Absolute rules at Problem Aware.**

| Rule | Value |
|---|---|
| CTA above the fold | NO — first CTA only after the Unique Mechanism section |
| Product named above the fold | NO — the headline is problem-driven |
| Price visible in first half | NO — wait until the offer section |
| Big Idea required | YES — essential; the idea reframes the problem in a way the prospect hasn't heard |
| Length range | Long (3000-10000+ words) |
| Emotional setup | Heavy and earned before any solution pivot |

**Headline DOES contain.**

- The problem in the prospect's exact words
- Optionally a hint at the way out (without spoiling the mechanism)
- A signal that the writer comprehends the prospect's experience

**Headline DOES NOT contain.**

- Product name
- Offer
- Generic claims
- Solutions without proof
- CTA

**Chain of beliefs minimum required.** Beliefs 1-8 from the chain library — "the problem exists", "the problem is priority", "the problem is worsening", "the problem won't resolve on its own", "your previous understanding was incomplete", "what you tried failed because it didn't address the mechanism", etc. PLUS beliefs 9-17 to bridge to your solution. See `chain-of-beliefs.md`.

**Typical headline examples (illustrative).**

- "If you wake up tired no matter how much you sleep, doctors are now linking it to [specific cause] — and here's the [mechanism] that addresses it"
- "[Problem] isn't your fault. Here's what's actually causing it — and the [mechanism] that finally fixes it"

### Level 5 — Unaware

**Definition.** The prospect doesn't recognize the problem or desire — either because they genuinely haven't felt it, because they won't admit it to themselves, because the problem is too vague to articulate, or because the topic is socially taboo.

**Diagnostic signals.**

- No searches related to the problem (they're not looking)
- They identify with a lifestyle, value system, or community — but not with the problem
- The problem is socially uncomfortable to acknowledge (body odor, financial failure, sexual dysfunction, parenting failures)
- The desire is so general it resists being summed up ("I want a better life")
- Most challenging — and most rewarding — level to convert

**Strategic approach.**

Identification headline. The copy does NOT sell, does NOT promise, does NOT name the product. It picks the prospect out of the crowd by echoing an emotion, an identity, a moment, a story.

**Inviolable negative rules at this level (HARD).**

- NEVER mention price in headline
- NEVER mention product name in headline
- NEVER mention product function in headline
- NEVER mention the desire in headline
- NEVER mention the problem in headline
- NEVER make a claim in headline

The headline's only job is to sell the **next line**. The next line sells the line after. Awareness builds layer by layer, paragraph by paragraph.

The lead is fully indirect. The path is:
1. Identification (an emotion, image, story, or universal moment they relate to)
2. Growing problem awareness (slowly surface the issue)
3. Solution exists
4. Your product

The product is mentioned deep into the body, never in the opening.

Several sub-patterns are commonly used at this level:

- Giving words to a hidden dream
- Exploiting a hidden fear
- Leading into an unacceptable problem via a universally accepted image
- Projecting a hidden desire that can't be stated bluntly
- Using a common resentment or unvoiced protest
- Projecting an ultimate triumph the prospect identifies with
- Projecting the result of a problem to attract people who reject the direct statement
- Projecting the result of an accomplishment to attract people who would be frightened by the work implied

These are all variations of the same principle: do not name the desire or problem; let the prospect identify with the surface element and walk themselves into the realization.

**Camouflage frames at the headline and lead level.**

A second axis of design crosses the psychological sub-patterns above: how the piece is FRAMED. Unaware copy almost always camouflages itself as something other than a sales piece, so the prospect engages without the defensive reflex that direct sales signals trigger. The frame lives at the headline and lead level — it sets the genre the reader thinks they're reading.

The recurring frames:

- **News / scoop frame** — the piece reads like a current-events article. The headline could plausibly appear in a magazine or on a news site. The reader engages out of news curiosity, not sales interest. Often hooks to a recent event, a public figure, a date, a place.
- **Scientific discovery frame** — a new study, a recent finding, a breakthrough attributed to a named institution or researcher. The headline reads as "what science just revealed". Particularly effective in health, finance, performance, and cognitive-improvement niches.
- **Rediscovered or suppressed knowledge frame** — old wisdom that worked for decades or centuries, buried or forgotten, now resurfaced. Or knowledge that "the industry doesn't want you to find". Leverages the appeal of restored / hidden truth. Works when the audience already feels modern solutions have failed them.
- **Conspiracy / hidden-interest frame** — explicit accusation that powerful interests (industry, regulators, lobbies, legacy authorities) have suppressed the truth. Higher-risk frame: it lands strongly in niches where the prospect already distrusts authority (alternative health, off-the-grid finance, some political-adjacent verticals), and fails in niches where it reads as fringe. The Strategist surfaces this risk explicitly before locking the frame.
- **Personal story / memoir frame** — the piece is told as a first-person account: the founder's struggle, a customer's transformation, a witnessed event. Reads as a story, not a pitch. The selling enters through narrative inevitability, never as announcement.

Frames combine with the psychological sub-patterns above — they are not alternatives. A "news / scoop" frame can activate "exploiting a hidden fear" (e.g., a news article about an emerging health risk). A "rediscovered knowledge" frame can activate "giving words to a hidden dream" (e.g., an ancient practice that delivers a result modernity stopped offering). A "scientific discovery" frame can activate "projecting an ultimate triumph". The frame is the wrapper that earns the open; the psychological sub-pattern is the engine inside that earns the conversion.

Pick a frame BEFORE writing the headline. Most failures at Unaware come from writing the body first and then trying to retrofit a frame on top — the frame must be the headline's native language from sentence one, or the camouflage breaks and the piece reads as advertising.

**What the copy MUST NOT do.**

- Anything direct
- Anything in a hurry
- Use the structure of a Most Aware or Product Aware piece

**Asset implications.**

- Format: long-form storytelling content, native advertorial, camouflage piece, narrative VSL, story-led email
- Length: very long (5000-15000+ words for a full piece)
- Education depth: complete — the entire awareness journey from zero
- Emotional setup: dominant; the copy is mostly emotional, with logic deployed late

**Operational page blueprint.**

The longest and most camouflaged structure. The page sells nothing in its first half — it builds identification, then surfaces the problem, then the solution, then the product. Every direct sales signal in the opening half kills the piece. The structure is narrative; the chain of beliefs is installed through story progression.

| # | Functional block | What it installs | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Above the fold | Identification — the prospect sees themselves in the surface element | Headline mirrors an emotion, a moment, an identity, a universal scene. NO product, NO problem, NO desire, NO price, NO claim. The headline sells the next line, nothing more. |
| 2 | Lead (extended) | Builds recognition; the prospect identifies further | Story, scene, sensory immersion. The reader is being drawn in by the narrative, not by the offer. |
| 3 | Problem emergence | Ring: "I have a problem" | The problem surfaces gradually through the narrative. The reader arrives at the realization on their own, not by being told. |
| 4 | Solution exists | Ring: "Solutions exist" | The category of solution is introduced — still no product. |
| 5 | Problem mechanism | Ring: "Here is the real cause" | The "missing 1%" — the new understanding of why the problem persists. |
| 6 | Unique Mechanism | Ring: "There is a unique way to solve it" | The UM is introduced. Far into the body. By this point the reader has earned every belief on the path. |
| 7 | Benefits + reason-why (repeatable) | Dimensionalizes the new desire | Each benefit grounded in mechanism + proof. |
| 8 | Offer detail | Ring: "I deserve this and can afford it; procrastinating could harm me" | Descriptive, full. The offer is being seen for the first time. |
| 9 | FAQ | Residual objections | Standard. |
| 10 | Footer | Standard | Compliance + trust elements. |

The blocks are functional, not fixed slots. Unaware pieces are the longest in the system and the section count is highest because every ring from 1 onward must be installed through narrative.

**Absolute rules at Unaware (HARD — violating these kills the piece).**

| Rule | Value |
|---|---|
| CTA above the fold | NEVER |
| Product named above the fold | NEVER |
| Price visible in first half | NEVER |
| Product reference in first half | NEVER |
| Brand name prominent in opening | NEVER (brand emerges late, if at all) |
| Big Idea required | ALWAYS — this is the level that absolutely demands one |
| Length range | Very long (5000-15000+ words) |
| Emotional setup | Dominant; logic deployed late |

**Headline DOES contain.**

- An emotion, identity, moment, image, or universal scene the prospect recognizes
- Curiosity opening only — never a claim or a promise

**Headline DOES NOT contain.**

- Product, price, problem, desire, function, claim — all forbidden
- Anything that signals "this is selling something"

**Critical caveat.** Unaware copy requires a senior copywriter and a real swipe reference. The structural blueprint above is the skeleton; the execution requires craft that cannot be reduced to a template. The piece lives or dies on the quality of the identification.

**Chain of beliefs minimum required.** Full chain from belief 1 onwards, in sequence. The piece installs every belief from "the problem exists" through to "I deserve this solution and can afford it." See `chain-of-beliefs.md`.

**Typical headline examples (illustrative).**

- "Why men crack..." (classic example — does not name the product, problem, or desire; uses universal identification)
- "They laughed when I sat down at the piano — but when I started to play!" (classic — identification through triumph projection)
- "Within the curve of a woman's arm" (classic — universally accepted image leading into a taboo problem)

## Selecting the awareness level — a copywriter-led decision

> **Inviolable rule.** The awareness level for a funnel is ALWAYS selected by the copywriter, never autonomously by the Strategist. The same product can run across multiple funnels at different awareness levels — cold acquisition often targets Problem Aware or Unaware, warm retargeting typically targets Product Aware, customer-list emails target Most Aware. Only the copywriter knows which segment THIS specific funnel is meant for. The Strategist's job is to gather signals, present them clearly, and ASK the copywriter to choose — never to finalize the choice on its own.

The Strategist runs this during Phase 1 of brief building, immediately after `mass-desire.md` has produced the (desire ↔ performance) pair.

### Step 1 — Gather signals from the brand wiki

Pull from:

- `brands/<brand>/research/market-awareness.md` → the dominant awareness level from deep research (if available) and the % distribution across levels
- `brands/<brand>/research/unified-brief.md` → Section "Market — awareness and segments"
- `brands/<brand>/avatars/<segment>.md` → frontmatter field `dominant_awareness`
- `brands/<brand>/research/competitor-research.md` → competitor messaging style (Most Aware ads vs Solution Aware ads vs Unaware advertorials tell you where competitors think the market sits)

Fallback: if `research/` is empty or missing → flag the gap to the copywriter and work from `avatars/` + `competitors/`.

### Step 2 — Apply diagnostic signals

Cross-reference the 5 levels' diagnostic signals (above) with what the brand wiki says about the market. The signals should converge on ONE dominant level.

If signals split between two adjacent levels, this is normal — markets have distributions, not single points. Identify the DOMINANT level (where the majority of prospects sit) and note the secondary level for split-funnel considerations.

### Step 3 — Present the findings to the copywriter

The Strategist consolidates what the data says and presents it to the copywriter — never as a closed recommendation, always as a proposal for input:

- What the dominant level appears to be in the data
- The distribution if it's split (e.g., "60% Problem Aware, 25% Solution Aware")
- Any conflicts between sources (research vs avatar vs competitor messaging)
- The 2-3 awareness levels that could plausibly be targeted by THIS funnel depending on its strategic role:
  - Cold acquisition typically targets Problem Aware or Unaware
  - Warm retargeting typically targets Product Aware
  - Customer-list emails typically target Most Aware

### Step 4 — Ask the copywriter to select

The Strategist explicitly asks the copywriter to choose. Example phrasing:

> "From the data, the market sits dominantly at Problem Aware (60%) with a secondary Solution Aware segment (25%). This funnel could plausibly target either, depending on its role. The Problem Aware route means longer copy and heavier education; the Solution Aware route means a warmer audience and mechanism-led pitch. Which segment is THIS funnel for?"

The Strategist MAY include a recommendation with rationale, but never closes the selection on its own. If the copywriter has already specified the awareness level upfront (e.g., "this is the retargeting funnel for Product Aware"), the Strategist confirms — and re-checks the data isn't strongly contradicting it. If there's a contradiction, it surfaces it as a question, not a correction.

### Step 5 — Document the chosen level in the brief

Fill the brief's Awareness Level block — §3.3 of [funnel-brief](core/strategic-frameworks/funnel-brief.md), which owns the canonical block format. Do NOT redefine the block here.

Fields this file feeds into §3.3:

- **Selected level** — the copywriter's choice from Step 4
- **Audience temperature rationale** — Cold / Warm / Hot / Retargeting, derived from the funnel's strategic role (Step 3)
- **Education vs amplification route** — from the Diagnostic section of this file; drives the "Operational implications" (must do / must not do) lines of §3.3
- **Minimum rings required** — from the at-a-glance table + chain-of-beliefs.md §4
- **Beliefs already held / to install** — from the level's "Chain of beliefs minimum required" range, mapped to belief numbers

Optional lines worth annotating inside the §3.3 block:

- **Sources** — the files / sections from which the level was determined (Step 1), plus the % distribution if the market is split

This output feeds directly into:
- **Sophistication assessment** (next step — [unique-mechanism](core/strategic-frameworks/unique-mechanism.md) §2)
- **Chain of beliefs selection** (`chain-of-beliefs.md` — picks specific beliefs from the minimum range required for this level)
- **Specialist invocation** (`format-specialists/<format>-specialist.md` — the writing specialist reads this file's awareness-specific Operational page blueprint at brief time)

## The psychological wall — common mapping errors

**Error 1: assuming Most Aware because the brand is well-known.**
Brand recognition is not awareness of the product offer. A prospect can recognize a brand name and still be Product Aware (knows the category, evaluates options) or even Solution Aware (knows the desire, doesn't yet associate the brand category with it).

**Error 2: treating Solution Aware as Problem Aware (or vice versa).**
The two are easily confused. Diagnostic: Problem Aware feels the pain but doesn't believe solutions work. Solution Aware believes solutions can work and is evaluating which.

**Error 3: defaulting to "Problem Aware" because it's the safe middle.**
Problem Aware requires long-form, heavy emotional setup, and either Original Problem or New Better Solution route. If the market is actually Solution Aware, all that emotional setup wastes the prospect's time and erodes attention. Always verify before defaulting.

**Error 4: ignoring the splinter between funnels.**
A brand may have multiple avatars at different awareness levels. The funnel targets ONE avatar at ONE level. Don't average across them.

**Error 5: skipping Unaware because "no one is unaware these days".**
Unaware is the most common level for genuinely new categories, for taboo problems, and for high-end identification copy. Don't dismiss it because the broader internet has been exposed to many topics.
