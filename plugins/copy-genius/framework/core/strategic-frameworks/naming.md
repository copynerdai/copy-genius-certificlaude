# Naming

> Universal naming framework. Applies to **anything** in a campaign that benefits from a proprietary handle: brands, products, Unique Mechanisms, offers, bonuses, OTOs, guarantees, methodologies, processes, ingredients, certifications, campaigns, launches, taglines.
>
> **Consumer contract**
> Read by: the **Copy Genius Strategist** — at: any step where an asset needs a name and one isn't already provided by the brand or selected by the copywriter; most common caller: `unique-mechanism.md` §10 Step 6 (UM naming).
> Output: feeds the name fields of funnel-brief blocks — the UM name in §3.4, offer / bonus / guarantee names in §3.6 — [funnel-brief](core/strategic-frameworks/funnel-brief.md) owns the block formats; this file only explains how to derive the name.
> Load-bearing sections (always read): §5 (criteria sheet + banned constructs), §7 (brainstorming protocol), §8 (False Name gate), §9 (operational sequence), §10 (output format). >
> Core thesis: **"What has no name is a commodity."** Naming is the act of un-commoditizing an asset. It is technique, not pure creativity — sit-and-wait-for-inspiration produces bad names. Sit-with-paper-and-thesaurus-and-run-the-techniques produces great names.

---

## 1. Core principle

A nameless asset is a commodity. Commodities compete on price and disappear into the category. A named asset is a brand within itself — you can register it, repeat it, build memory around it, anchor copy to it.

This applies at every scale:

- A campaign without a campaign name dissolves into "the Black Friday offer". A campaign with a name (e.g., "The Open Doors Week") is its own event.
- A bonus called "My last live talk" creates zero buying urgency. The same bonus named *"The Atelier Method — Closed-Doors Edition"* creates curiosity, scarcity, and worth.
- A product called "the premium pillow" disappears among 1,000 identical pillows. *Sleepwave* (a fictional example pattern) becomes the category's named thing.
- A UM without a name floats in vacuum. A UM with a technical-sounding name (Method X.0, Protocol Y, [Greek-root]-tics) becomes a registrable asset and a persuasive anchor.

### Three operational truths

1. **Naming is technique, not creativity.** Pure brainstorming produces bad names except in the hands of someone who has internalized the techniques for years. For everyone else: paper + thesaurus + the 12 techniques below.
2. **Best names combine 2-4 techniques.** A name that operates on one level is acceptable; a name that operates on three levels (descriptive + alliterative + portmanteau, for example) is memorable.
3. **The name does the heavy lifting of memorability.** Repetition across the campaign compounds the asset — every mention of the named thing reinforces the prior context the prospect has built around it.

### What this file replaces

This file is invoked from `unique-mechanism.md` section 9 (Naming the UM) and from any other workflow that needs to produce a name. unique-mechanism.md §9 governs only WHEN the UM needs a name (by Type A/B/C); all UM-specific craft rules — required register, Technique 11 default, Greek/Latin roots, per-letter defensibility, search-clean test — live here in §6.3, together with the **brainstorming framework** that produces the candidate names.

## 3. The 12 techniques

### Technique 0 — Descriptive name (the default)

The simplest and often the most powerful. The name describes what the thing does, specifically and vividly.

**Rules**:

- The banned constructs apply (no superlatives, no vague positioning adjectives, no hollow abstractions) — the single canonical list lives in §5 "Banned constructs"
- Must be **specific** (a particular feature, not the whole category)
- Must be **vivid** (something the prospect can picture)
- Must be **hard to copy** (something a competitor can't claim without sounding like a follower)

**The "anyone could say this" test**: if a competitor could put the same word on their product without changing anything, the word is dead. *Superior Plumbing* fails because no plumber says they're inferior. *Best Pillow* fails because every pillow claims to be best.

**Strong descriptive names — pattern**:

- **Category-capturing compound**: a product whose name becomes the way people refer to the whole category (e.g., *Crock Pot*, *Fitbit*). High difficulty to achieve, massive payoff when achieved.
- **Animal-attribute compound**: an animal name imports its attributes (strength, speed, cunning, endurance). The product inherits the connotation: *Gorilla Tape* (= strength), *Red Bull* (= testosterone/force).
- **Promise compound**: the name says exactly what the product delivers. *Slim Cup* (= slimming cup). Best for products whose primary promise is concrete and visualizable.

**When descriptive fails**:

- The descriptive name is already taken or trademarked
- The descriptive name is too generic to differentiate (every product in the category is "the X for Y")
- The product is genuinely undifferentiated (no clear feature to anchor to) → consider technique 6

### Technique 1 — Alphabetization

A systematic creativity unlock. Take the key concept; iterate every letter of the alphabet in front of (or behind) it; listen for sound combinations that create something new.

**Process**:

1. Write the key word on paper (e.g., *Kung Fu* for a verbal-self-defense product)
2. Write the alphabet vertically next to it
3. Substitute the first letter (or add a letter prefix/suffix) and read aloud: *aung-fu, bung-fu, cung-fu… tung-fu* (→ *Tongue Fu*)
4. Stop when you hit a combination that *means something* in context
5. Adjust spelling if needed for clarity

**When to use**:

- Product naming when descriptive is taken
- Methodology / program naming
- When you have one core concept word and need to make it proprietary

**Example pattern**: a "yogurt-on-the-go" product → iterate *yo-gurt* → *go-gurt*. The result captures the on-the-go benefit AND keeps the category reference.

### Technique 2 — Customer quotes

Read testimonials, reviews, forums, social comments, sales-call transcripts. Listen for short vivid phrases customers use to describe the problem or the desired outcome. Those phrases are pre-installed in the target's mental dialogue — using them creates instant resonance.

**Process**:

1. Mine `brands/<brand>/testimonials.md`, `brands/<brand>/transcripts/`, `brands/<brand>/research/`, and external review platforms
2. Pull every short, emotionally-loaded phrase the customer naturally uses
3. Select the one that captures the dominant emotional state OR the dominant unspoken question
4. Use that phrase as the name / tagline / book title / webinar title

**Best for**: webinars, sales-letter headlines, book titles, lead-magnet names. Less common for product or UM names (customer language is usually too casual for proprietary-sounding handles).

**Example pattern**: a children's preventive-medicine webinar → the universal anxious-parent question becomes the title: *"Doctor, why is my child always sick?"*. Zero invention; the question already lives in the audience's head.

### Technique 3 — Borrowed authority

Take a well-known title, phrase, idiom, or cultural reference and graft your keyword onto it. The prospect's pre-existing recognition of the source transfers to your name.

**Process**:

1. Select the key word associated with the asset
2. Search:
   - Book titles (Amazon, Goodreads)
   - Song lyrics (lyrics platforms)
   - Film / TV titles (IMDB)
   - Proverbs, idioms, common sayings in the target's language
   - Generation-specific cultural references (calibrated to the avatar's age cohort)
3. Find one whose rhythm matches your concept
4. Fuse it with your keyword

**Caution — generation drift**: cultural references decay across age cohorts. A reference that resonates with one generation may be invisible to another. Verify the reference is alive in the avatar's cohort before locking the name.

**Pattern examples** (neutral): *"The [Subject] Chronicles"*, *"[Subject] in the City"*, *"The Devil Wears [Subject]"*.

### Technique 4 — Portmanteau / blend (macedonia)

Fuse two words into one. Among the highest-leverage techniques because the resulting word is:

- Memorable (the brain registers the combination)
- Often trademark-eligible (it's a coined word)
- Hard to copy (a competitor would need to invent their own blend)
- Multi-layered (carries both source meanings)

**Process**:

1. Identify the two concepts to fuse
2. Try multiple fusion points (front of A + back of B, middle of A + middle of B)
3. Read aloud — the blend must pronounce smoothly
4. Verify it doesn't accidentally mean something embarrassing in another language

**Pattern examples** (neutral): *Netflix* (internet + flicks), *Mobike* (move + bike), *Pinterest* (pin + interest), *Cowspiracy* (cows + conspiracy).

### Technique 5 — Contrarian

Take the most widespread belief in the target's category and affirm the opposite. The contrarian frame creates instant attention because it breaks pattern.

**Process**:

1. Identify the dominant belief / commonplace in the category (from competitor research and avatar materials)
2. Invert it as the name or title
3. Verify the inversion is **defensible** — the asset's content must actually deliver on the contrarian frame, otherwise it's clickbait and B4 (brand trustworthy) collapses

**When to use**:

- Book titles
- Course titles
- Lead-magnet titles
- Big Idea launches

**Pattern example** (generic): the category-wide belief is *"Money doesn't buy happiness"* → contrarian title: *"Money Buys Happiness"*. The brand must then defend the inversion in the body of the asset.

### Technique 6 — Personal metaphor (for desperate cases)

When the entrepreneur has no UM, no unique angle, no differentiation. The name pulls from their personal story — their hobby, their adversity, their idiosyncratic life-fact — and grafts it onto the product.

**Process — the 4-column paper**:

Take a sheet of paper (landscape). Divide it into 4 vertical columns:

| Product keywords | Hobby keywords | Greatest successes | Greatest adversities |
|---|---|---|---|
| [list] | [list] | [list] | [list] |

Look for combinations between the **product column** and the **other three columns**. A connection between a product keyword and a personal-life keyword is often the seed of a name that:

- Is impossible to copy (no competitor has the same life story)
- Carries identity (it's the entrepreneur's brand by definition)
- Generates explanatory hooks (the name becomes a story everyone wants told)

**When to use**: the technique of last resort. Use only when techniques 0-5 produce nothing AND techniques 7-9 produce nothing.

**Pattern example** (generic): a financial advisor with a golf passion → product keyword "investments" intersects hobby keyword "green" → title *"Go for the Green"*, with chapters mapped to the 18 golf holes. The golf metaphor wouldn't fit any other advisor's book.

### Technique 7 — Onomatopoeic sound

Build the name from a sound that evokes the product's essence.

**Process**:

1. Write product bullets (features + benefits)
2. Highlight the words that capture the strongest feature/benefit
3. Modify spelling to keep the sound but generate a new orthography
4. Test by reading aloud — the sound must feel like the product

**Pattern examples** (neutral): *Lyft* (lift), *Twitter* (the small bird sound of a small message), *Cheerios* (cheer up), *Roomba* (the rumba dance the robot looks like it's doing across the floor).

**When to use**: when you have a strong sensory or sound association the product evokes (movement, lightness, force, calm).

### Technique 8 — Alliteration

Repeat the initial consonant across two words of the name. The repetition makes the name easier to memorize, easier to repeat, and more melodic.

**Process**:

1. List product keywords + features + benefits
2. Identify the keyword that **can't be removed** without losing the product's identity
3. Combine that keyword with other words starting with the same letter
4. Use a thesaurus to find synonyms starting with that letter

**Pattern examples** (neutral): *Coca-Cola*, *Mickey Mouse*, *PayPal*, *Dunkin Donuts*, *Best Buy*, *Krispy Kreme*.

**Bonus technique — alliterating with the category name**: when the brand name + the category name share an initial letter, the public tends to say both together even when the brand never officially adopted that pairing. (*Carnival* the cruise line is just *Carnival*, but the public says *Carnival Cruises* because of the alliteration.) This is a free amplifier: if you can pick a brand initial that alliterates with your category word, the market repeats both for you.

### Technique 9 — Rhyme

Two rhyming words. The rhyme creates pleasing recall AND signals deliberate construction (rhyme is hard to fake casually).

**Caution**: rhymes go wrong fast. A forced rhyme that sacrifices clarity makes the brand sound like a child's product. Use sparingly and only when the rhyme genuinely reinforces the meaning.

**Pattern examples** (neutral): *Stub Hub*, *Tech Deck*, *Fitbit* (rhyme + portmanteau + descriptive — operates on three levels).

### Technique 11 — Acronyms (the UM technique)

The technique designed for **Unique Mechanisms** specifically. An acronym sounds technical, scientific, formal — the right register for a UM, where Type C (Transubstantiated, see unique-mechanism.md §3) demands a name that reads as proprietary engineering rather than marketing copy.

**Process**:

1. Write all the words that genuinely relate to the product / process / method (not aspirational — actual operational components)
2. For each word, list 2-3 synonyms (use a thesaurus)
3. Pull the first letter of each candidate and start combining
4. Iterate until you find a combination whose letters spell something that:
   - Sounds like a real word or a credible scientific code
   - Doesn't return existing strong results on a Google search
   - Has a memorable rhythm (most acronyms benefit from periods between letters for emphasis: *D.A.R.D.O.*, *D.R.E.A.M.*)
5. Validate that each letter corresponds to a real, defensible component of the method (otherwise it's False Mechanism territory — see §8)

**Pattern**:

- *D.R.E.A.M.* method (Discovery / Refinement / Execution / Acceleration / Mastery — or any defensible 5-word breakdown)
- *Method X.[N]* / *Protocol [Code]* / *System [Greek root]*-tics

**Greek and Latin roots add authority**: an acronym or compound built from classical roots reads as scientific even when invented. Pattern: take a Greek anatomical or process word (e.g., *opis* = back/buttock; *kine* = movement; *thermo* = heat) and combine with a method noun (*synergy*, *protocol*, *method*). Result: *Opisthetic Synergy*, *Kinetic Protocol*, *Thermotropic Method*.

## 4. Combining techniques

The strongest names operate on multiple layers simultaneously. A name that's *only* descriptive is acceptable; a name that's descriptive AND alliterative AND a portmanteau is memorable for years.

### Examples of layered names (neutral, well-known)

| Name | Layers operating |
|---|---|
| *Fitbit* | Rhyme + portmanteau (fit + bit) + descriptive |
| *Coca-Cola* | Alliteration + descriptive (coca leaf + kola nut) + category embedding |
| *Mickey Mouse* | Alliteration + character archetype + descriptive (a mouse named Mickey) |
| *M&M's* | Alliteration + descriptive (two founders' initials) + memorable visual |
| *Netflix* | Portmanteau (internet + flicks) + descriptive |
| *Carnival* (the cruise line) | Descriptive of experience + alliteration-with-category (the public says "Carnival Cruises") |

### Combination rule

When evaluating a candidate name, check: how many techniques does it deploy? More techniques = more memorability, more multi-layer reading, more durability.

A name should aspire to at least **2 layers**. Single-layer names work but are less defensible against competitor copies.

## 5. Rules of good naming (the criteria sheet)

When validating any candidate name, run it through this checklist:

### Mandatory rules

1. **Specificity** > genericity. The name says something specific, not something generic.
2. **Vividness** > abstraction. The name evokes a picture, not a concept.
3. **Hard to copy**. A competitor can't put the same word on their product without sounding like a follower.
4. **Short** > long. 1-3 syllables ideal for brand and product. Up to 5 syllables acceptable for methodology names.
5. **Pronounceable** in the target's language. Read aloud — does it stumble?
6. **Spellable** after one hearing. The audience can write it after one mention without confusion.
7. **Searchable**. Google the name. If the first page is dominated by an existing competitor or an unrelated established meaning, change it.
8. **Trademark-clear** (preliminary check). Search the relevant trademark registers (USPTO for US, EUIPO for EU, UIBM for Italy) for direct conflicts in the asset's category.
9. **No accidental meaning in adjacent languages**. If the brand will operate in multiple markets, search the name in each target language for unintended meanings.

### Soft rules (apply context-dependently)

10. **Matches the brand's communication register**. A luxury skincare UM name shouldn't sound like industrial machinery; a fitness coach UM name shouldn't sound like pharmaceutical research (unless the brand wants that register).
11. **Uses the target's vocabulary** where applicable. If the customer says "X", consider including X. If the customer says "the X-thing", consider naming it "X-thing".
12. **Operates on multiple layers**. Aim for 2+ techniques per name.

### Banned constructs

- Superlatives: *Top*, *Pro*, *Best*, *Premium*, *Elite*, *Ultimate*, *Supreme*, *Master* (in titles, not in proper nouns)
- Vague positioning adjectives: *Reliable*, *Trustworthy*, *Smart*, *Innovative*, *Advanced*, *Next-Gen*
- Hollow abstractions: *Solutions*, *Group*, *International*, *Worldwide*, *Global*
- Generic acronyms (when the underlying words are themselves generic — *BRT* for *Bartolini* read as worse than the original full name because the acronym carries no recognition value)
- "Aspirational" taglines with no proof anchor (these work for billion-dollar brands with decade-long ad budgets; for everyone else they read as empty)

## 6. Asset-specific naming guidance

### 6.1 Brand naming

- **Short**: 1-3 syllables ideal
- **Memorable**: at least 2 techniques layered
- **Hard to copy**: techniques 4, 6, 8 produce the most copy-resistant names
- **Trademark-eligible**: techniques 4 (portmanteau) and 1 (alphabetization) produce coined words that are trademark-strongest
- **Embed the category** if possible (technique 8 + category): *Coca-Cola*, *Carnival Cruises (de facto)*, *Mickey Mouse*
- **Avoid acronyms as primary brand names** — they read as cold and lose the recognition value of the underlying words. Acronyms work for UMs, not for consumer-facing brands.

### 6.2 Product naming

- Should represent the **primary promise** of the product, vividly
- Should aspire to **category capture** — a name so well-fit that the public eventually calls every similar product by it (*Crock Pot*, *Slim Cup*, *Fitbit*)
- Compound names (technique 4) often work well — they capture the promise AND signal proprietary construction
- "Leadership" tags ("the #1 X") don't belong in product names — leadership comes from clarity, not from claiming it

### 6.3 Unique Mechanism naming

UMs operate by different naming rules than products. The UM name must signal **proprietary engineering / scientific protocol / formal method** — not retail product.

- **Technique 11 (acronyms) is the default**. *Method [ACRONYM]*, *Protocol [ACRONYM]*, *[ACRONYM] System*
- **Greek and Latin roots** add immediate authority — invented compounds like *Opisthetic Synergy* read as scientific
- **Numbers and codes** signal version control / engineering — *Method 7.0*, *Protocol X-12*
- **Avoid retail vocabulary** — words like *Premium*, *Plus*, *Ultra* drop the UM out of the engineering register and into the retail register
- **Search before locking**: Google the UM name. The first page should return no significant competitor results. If it does, the UM name is already in someone else's territory.
- **The name must correspond to actual operational components** — if it's an acronym, each letter must defend a real, distinct part of the method. Hollow acronyms are False Mechanism territory (see unique-mechanism.md §5).

Cross-reference: unique-mechanism.md §9 governs **when** the UM needs naming (by Type A/B/C); this file governs **how** to brainstorm and select the name.

### 6.4 Offer / bonus / OTO / campaign naming

These often get neglected — the marketer focuses on the product name and leaves the bonus called *"My last live talk"*. That's a commodity bonus regardless of how good the actual content is.

- **Bonus**: pretend the bonus is a product in its own right. Apply the product-naming rules.
- **OTO**: avoid the word *upsell* in customer-facing copy. Name the OTO as if it's an exclusive product unlocked at this specific moment.
- **Campaign / launch**: don't call it "Black Friday Offer" or "Summer Sale". Name the campaign as an event. *The Open Doors Week*, *The Atelier Drop*, *The Founding Members' Window*. Techniques 3 (borrowed authority), 5 (contrarian), 9 (rhyme) often fit campaign-naming.
- **Guarantee**: don't call it "30-day money-back". Apply technique 0 + 4: *The 100-Day Match-or-Refund-5x Guarantee*, *The Mechanism Guarantee* (when the guarantee is tied to the UM specifically).

### 6.5 Tagline / slogan (adjacent territory)

Tagline construction is adjacent to naming and shares some rules. A short note here; full tagline methodology (if needed) would warrant its own file.

- **Must describe the differentiating strength** — not aspirational mood
- **Use the customer's vocabulary** — listen for how customers describe the product in 3 words; those 3 words are often the tagline
- **Avoid aspirational** unless the brand has the ad budget to install the aspiration (billion-dollar brands can; small businesses cannot)
- **Anchor to the UM where possible** — a tagline that names the UM benefit reads as proof, not aspiration
- **Test by removing the brand name** — if the tagline could attach to any brand in the category, it's dead

## 7. The brainstorming protocol (operational)

This is the actual workflow the Strategist executes when invoked to brainstorm a name.

### Step 1 — Gather inputs

From the caller and the brand wiki:

- The **asset type** (brand / product / UM / offer / bonus / campaign / etc.)
- The **primary promise** or function of the asset
- The **target avatar's language** (from avatar materials, transcripts, testimonials)
- The **brand's communication register** (from `brands/<brand>/brand.md`)
- The **competitor naming patterns** (from `brands/<brand>/competitors/*.md`) — to know what to avoid sounding like
- The **constraints** (legal / trademark / language / domain availability if relevant)
- For **UMs specifically**: the UMP/UMS articulation (from `unique-mechanism.md` output) — the acronym letters must defend real components of the UMP/UMS
- For **technique 6** (personal metaphor): the entrepreneur's hobby / story / adversity material (from `brands/<brand>/brand.md` and transcripts)

### Step 2 — Run technique 0 first (descriptive)

Always start here. If a strong descriptive name passes all criteria (§5), there's no need to invent.

### Step 2-bis — UM branch

If the asset is a **UM** → skip the technique-0-first rule and start from **Technique 11 (acronyms)**; §6.3 governs the register and craft rules. The "Technique 0 first" default applies to products, offers, bonuses, campaigns, and funnels — not to UMs.

### Step 3 — If descriptive fails or is too weak, run techniques 1-9 in order

For each technique that fits the asset type (use §6 guidance), generate 3-10 candidates. Don't pre-filter — generate broadly first.

- Technique 4 (portmanteau) and technique 8 (alliteration) are the highest-yield for general brand/product use
- Technique 11 (acronym) is the default for UMs
- Technique 6 (personal metaphor) is the last-resort technique

### Step 4 — Combine candidates across techniques

Look at the generated candidates and ask: which candidates can be improved by layering a second or third technique on top?

- A descriptive name + alliteration is stronger than either alone
- A portmanteau + descriptive is stronger than either alone
- A rhyme + portmanteau + descriptive (like *Fitbit*) operates on three layers

### Step 5 — Stress-test the shortlist

For each candidate that survives Step 4, run:

1. **Say it aloud** 5 times. Does it flow? Does it stumble?
2. **Write it twice** by hand. Does the spelling feel natural? Is the spelling memorable after one exposure?
3. **Search it**: Google + the relevant trademark register + the relevant language markets (if international). Does it return clean? Is it taken?
4. **Test the meaning** in adjacent languages if the brand will operate in multiple markets.
5. **Test against the criteria sheet (§5)** — every mandatory rule must pass.

### Step 6 — Cull to 3-5 finalists

The Strategist's output is a shortlist of 3-5 candidates, ordered by the Strategist's preference, with rationale per candidate.

### Step 7 — Present to the copywriter

For each finalist, show:

- The name
- The technique(s) used
- The rationale (why this name fits the asset and the brand)
- The multi-layer reading (what additional meanings the name carries)
- The stress-test results
- The Strategist's recommendation (optional — Strategist may recommend with rationale but never closes)

### Step 8 — ASK the copywriter to select

The copywriter selects from the shortlist OR rejects all and rebriefs. The Strategist documents the final selection in the relevant artifact (brief, UM block, offer page, etc.).

## Deployment rules (post-selection — relocated from Technique 10)

Once a name is selected, it must be repeated across the campaign, redundantly and deliberately, so the audience encodes it.

**Rules**:

- Every piece of the funnel mentions the named asset at least twice
- Sales-call scripts repeat the name explicitly
- Customer-facing communications use the name in place of generic referents ("the program" → use the named program name)
- Internal materials (employee training, partner briefings) use the same name — internal/external consistency builds external authority

The single biggest naming failure mode is: name created, name not repeated. The asset has a name on the launch day and then the team reverts to "the program / the offer / the bonus / the method". The name dies of underuse.

## 8. False naming — the trap

A False Name is a name with the *shape* of a brand but none of the substance: a generic descriptor dressed up, an acronym whose letters defend nothing, an aspirational tag that the brand can't back up.

### Symptoms

- **Hollow acronyms**: the letters spell something but the words behind the letters are generic or invented post-hoc to fit the acronym
- **Anyone-could-claim descriptors**: *Best X*, *Pro Y*, *Elite Z*, *Premium Q*
- **Trademark-weak generic names**: words that any competitor can register because they're descriptive of a category property
- **Aspirational taglines with no proof anchor**: "We make life better" / "Innovation that matters" / "The future, today"
- **Names that need a paragraph to explain**: if you need 80 words of context for the audience to understand why the name fits, the name fails

### Why it's worse than a missing name

A weak name compounds *anti*-memorability: each repetition reinforces the wrong association, or no association at all. The audience encodes "generic-sounding thing" alongside the brand. Over time the brand IS the generic-sounding thing in their head.

A missing name leaves the asset as a commodity but at least doesn't damage the brand. A weak name actively trains the audience to dismiss the brand.

### How to avoid

- Run the criteria checklist (§5) without shortcuts
- **Hard gate**: if the candidate passes <80% of the mandatory rules (i.e. scores ≤7/9 on the 9-rule criteria sheet in §5) → kill it and run more techniques. 8/9 is the minimum score that survives the gate. A killed candidate never enters the shortlist presented to the copywriter
- Don't accept the first decent name — generate 20+ candidates before culling
- Always check the Google + trademark stress test before locking
- If the entrepreneur loves a name that fails the checklist, the Strategist surfaces the weaknesses to the copywriter and asks the copywriter to make the call (copywriter-led principle)

## 9. Operational sequence (Strategist contract)

Like every Strategist activity, naming is **GATHER → PRESENT → RECOMMEND → ASK** — the Strategist never closes a name on its own.

### Step 1 — Receive the request

From `unique-mechanism.md` §10 Step 6 (most common caller), from a brief-building step (when an offer/bonus/campaign needs a name), or from the copywriter directly.

### Step 2 — Gather constraints

Per §7 Step 1.

### Step 3 — Run the brainstorming protocol (§7)

### Step 4 — Present the shortlist with rationale

### Step 5 — Recommend one (optional, with rationale)

### Worked example (excerpt — naming a UM)

```
NAMING — UNIQUE MECHANISM

  Asset: UM for a hormonal-training fitness program (women's market)
  Caller: unique-mechanism.md §10 Step 6
  Constraints: scientific register required (Type C UM), Italian-language primary market, must not collide with existing fitness program names

  ─── Candidate 1 ───
    Name: "Protocollo T.R.E."
    Technique(s) used: 11 (acronym) + 0 (descriptive — "Protocollo" anchors register)
    Multi-layer reading: T = Testosterone phase, R = hormone Reset, E = Energy unlock; the acronym also reads as the Italian number "tre" (three), reinforcing the 3-phase structure
    Rationale: Acronym structure provides scientific register; the 3-phase mapping is defensible (each letter corresponds to a real protocol component); the Italian double-reading adds memorability
    Stress-test results:
      Pronounceable: yes (Italian-natural)
      Spellable: yes
      Searchable: clean (no existing fitness program with this acronym)
      Trademark check: preliminary clean — full IP search recommended before launch
      Multi-language check: "Tre" in Italian reads as "three" — bonus layer
    Criteria checklist score: 9/9

  ─── Candidate 2 ───
    Name: "Metodo Endokinetico"
    Technique(s) used: 0 (descriptive) + Greek root combination
    Multi-layer reading: endo- = internal (Greek), -kinetic = movement-based; the compound reads as scientific-internal-movement protocol
    Rationale: Greek-root construction provides authority; "endokinetico" doesn't collide with existing fitness or medical terminology
    Stress-test results:
      Pronounceable: yes
      Spellable: moderate — five syllables, less natural on first hearing
      Searchable: clean
      Trademark check: preliminary clean
    Criteria checklist score: 8/9 (slight weakness on spellability after first hearing)

  ─── Candidate 3 — KILLED by the §8 gate (shown to demonstrate the gate in action) ───
    Name: "Sistema H.E.R."
    Technique(s) used: 11 (acronym) + 3 (borrowed authority — the acronym doubles as the English pronoun "her", importing pre-existing recognition for the gendered target)
    Multi-layer reading: H = Hormone, E = Energy, R = Renewal; the acronym also reads as the English pronoun "her", anchoring the gendered target
    Rationale: Strong identity-fit for women's market; English-Italian hybrid is consistent with the brand register
    Stress-test results:
      Pronounceable: yes
      Spellable: yes
      Searchable: minor noise (the English pronoun creates SEO collision)
      Trademark check: needs careful IP search — single-syllable English words often conflict
    Criteria checklist score: 7/9 (search collision; trademark risk)
    Gate result: 7/9 = 77.8% < 80% threshold (§8) → candidate KILLED. It does NOT enter the shortlist;
    the Strategist runs more techniques to replace it. Listed here only to show the gate working.

  Strategist recommendation: Candidate 1 (Protocollo T.R.E.) — best stress-test profile, defensible per-letter mapping, dual-language reading bonus. (Recommendation only — copywriter selects.)
```
