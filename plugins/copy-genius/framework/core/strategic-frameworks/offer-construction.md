## 1. Core principle

The offer is the foundation of the campaign. Before the Big Idea, before the headline, before the lead — the campaign begins with **what is being sold, at what price, on what terms, with what additional elements that make the prospect feel they cannot refuse**.

Two operational truths:

1. **A strong offer with imperfect copy will work. A perfect copy with a mediocre offer will not.** Conversion follows the offer first; copy amplifies a real offer but cannot rescue a weak one.
2. **Working on the offer is the shortest path to making a campaign convert.** A week spent sharpening the offer outperforms a week spent polishing the copy.

### The two failure modes the Strategist guards against

**Failure mode A — passive acceptance**: the brand says *"sell my product as it is"*, and the copywriter accepts. The product is commodity-grade, the bonuses are afterthoughts, the price is naked, the guarantee is generic. The campaign launches and underperforms; the brand blames the copy.

**Failure mode B — leaky offer**: the brand has built an offer but several of the 7 components (§2) are weak or missing. The Strategist's job in evaluation mode is to identify the leak before the campaign launches.

### What the Strategist owes the copywriter

- An honest evaluation of the existing offer against the 7 components
- Specific, actionable improvements (not vague "consider strengthening the guarantee")
- A clear flag when the offer is not yet strong enough to support the campaign — and a recommendation to pause copy work until the offer is fixed

## 2. The 7 components of an offer

Every offer in direct response is composed of these 7 components. If only one is present, there is no offer — there is a price tag.

| # | Component | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Product / service** | The core thing being sold |
| 2 | **Benefits** | What the buyer gains — broken into Functional, Experiential, Emotional (see §3.2) |
| 3 | **Price + terms** | Cost AND how/when payment happens (installments, financing, returns, delivery, refund mechanics) |
| 4 | **Bonuses** | Complementary deliverables that elevate the experience and stack perceived value |
| 5 | **Risk inversion** | The guarantee — plus credibility elements that reduce the buyer's perceived risk |
| 6 | **Scarcity / urgency** | A reason the offer cannot wait (deadline, stock limit, batch size, cohort size) |
| 7 | **Call to action** | What to do next, in what order, with what expectation |

A complete offer has all 7. An offer missing 2 or more is structurally weak and should be flagged before the copy work begins.

## 3. Component deep-dives

### 3.1 Product / service

The core deliverable. Often the only thing the entrepreneur thinks they're selling — which is the root cause of most weak offers. The product is the *trigger*, not the offer itself; the offer is everything wrapped around the product.

**Evaluation checks**:

- Is the product fully described, with technical details where they matter and outcome details where they matter more?
- Is the deliverable specified (digital / physical / hybrid / service / subscription)?
- Is the delivery method specified (shipping / instant download / cohort start date / on-demand)?
- Is the product clearly distinct from competitor products on at least one defensible dimension?

If the product is genuinely commodity-grade (no defensible differentiation), the offer must compensate via bonuses, guarantee, and price+terms structuring — see §3.4 and §3.5.

### 3.2 Benefits — the 3 types

Every promise made in the campaign must be **tied to a specific element of the offer**. A benefit floating without an anchor in the deliverables is a False Benefit and a credibility leak.

**Functional benefits** — tied to features of the product or service. The basics: what it is, what it does, what it produces.

Example pattern: *"Includes 17 hours of recorded training, a 200-page workbook, and lifetime access to the platform."*

**Experiential benefits** — describe the *experience* of using the product. Multi-sensory language (sight, sound, touch, taste, smell). Cold technical description ("heated seats") is replaced by experiential ("the warmth radiates from your lower back up through your shoulders, so on a cold morning you slide in and feel like you're already home").

**Emotional benefits** — anchor an emotion: comfort, vindication, expectation, curiosity, belonging, relief, pride. For abstract/intangible products (spiritual training, identity work, status goods), emotional benefits carry most of the weight.

**The promise → element rule**

For every promise the copy makes, the Strategist must be able to point at a specific component of the offer that delivers it.

- Promise of "deep mechanism understanding" → must be tied to a specific deliverable (a video module, a workbook chapter, a 1:1 call).
- Promise of "transformation" → must be tied to an element that produces transformation (the protocol, the live event, the support community).

A promise without an element is a hollow promise. The campaign loses on the offer evaluation, not on the copy.

**Evaluation check**:

For each strong promise the brand wants to make, list:

- The promise
- The element of the offer that fulfills it (which bonus / which module / which feature)
- The benefit type (F / Ex / Em — Functional / Experiential / Emotional)

If a row has no element, EITHER add an element to the offer OR drop the promise from the copy. Don't write the campaign with unanchored promises.

### 3.3 Price + terms

Price is what is charged. Terms cover how/when payment happens, returns mechanics, delivery mechanics, what's included in the price vs. what's extra.

**Operational components of price + terms**:

| Component | What to specify |
|---|---|
| **List price** | The "anchor" price the offer discounts FROM (or stacks bonuses against) |
| **Offer price** | What the buyer actually pays |
| **Payment method** | Single payment / installments / financing / subscription |
| **Currency + tax inclusion** | Especially important for cross-border offers |
| **Refund mechanics** | Tied to the guarantee — see §3.5 |
| **Delivery mechanics** | Physical shipping, digital access, cohort start, time to first deliverable |
| **What's included** | Vs. what's extra (often a leak — buyer feels nickel-and-dimed) |

**Two strategic patterns for the offer price**

**Pattern A — Sell money at a discount (the stack)**

Don't discount the list price. Build the perceived value via bonuses, then offer everything for a fraction of the stacked value.

Pattern: *"List price of the book: €X. Plus Bonus 1 (€Y value). Plus Bonus 2 (€Z value). Plus Bonus 3 (€W value). Total value: €(X+Y+Z+W). Today, you pay €X."*

The buyer pays the list price of the core product but feels they receive €(Y+Z+W) extra for free. Works in all info-first businesses (events, courses, books, webinars).

**Pattern B — Real discount with stated reason**

If a real discount is being offered (not a stacked-bonus pattern), the reason must be stated explicitly. In Italian and Latin markets, an unexplained discount triggers the "where's the catch?" reflex.

Patterns that justify a real discount:

- Closeout / inventory clearance (with a reason: warehouse tax cost, expiring stock, end of season)
- Launch pricing for a new product (with a reason: pre-launch validation)
- Loyalty / customer-tier discount (with a reason: rewarding existing customers)
- Bundle discount (with a reason: bulk purchase economics)

A discount without a stated reason damages B4 (brand trustworthy) — see `chain-of-beliefs.md` §2.

**Negotiating price within the copy — the techniques**

These are operational levers the offer uses to make a high price feel justified:

1. **The "why" game** — operationally, the Strategist asks the entrepreneur *"why this price, why these terms, why this product configuration?"*. Most entrepreneurs take their own price logic for granted. Surfacing it produces the material that justifies the price in copy.

2. **Price decomposition** — break the price into per-day, per-hour, per-use units. €1,000/year → €2.74/day → "less than a coffee a day".

3. **Price dimensionalization** — anchor the price against something the avatar already pays without thinking. "€30,000 = a year of a dishwasher's salary in Switzerland" / "€50,000 = a single 30-second TV ad slot" / "€500 = what you'd spend on a single yacht-bottle of champagne". The anchor must be CALIBRATED to the avatar (premium anchors for premium buyers; everyday anchors for mass-market).

4. **Stacking against the buyer's loss** — calculate the cost of NOT buying. "Every month without [solution] costs you €X in [time/money/opportunity]; €X × 12 = €Y/year". The price becomes a discount against the loss-trajectory.

5. **Comparative table** — side-by-side comparison with competitor products or with the brand's own tiers (Standard vs. Premium vs. VIP — see §7).

**Cultural calibration**

- **Anglo-American markets**: respond to bold price + benefit claims; comfortable with discounts at face value
- **Italian / Latin markets**: more skeptical; discounts must be explained; trust must be built before price disclosure; bundle/stack patterns outperform raw discounts

### 3.4 Bonuses

Bonuses are the lever that turns a price-tag into an offer. A campaign without bonuses is almost always weaker than the same campaign with 3-5 well-built bonuses.

**The single biggest bonus failure mode**: the entrepreneur gives "what's left over" — a recording from an old event, an unrelated interview, content that was about to be deleted anyway. **The leftover bonus offers the same benefit as the main product** (just repackaged) and adds zero perceived value.

**Bonus construction principles**

A real bonus must:

1. **Offer a complementary benefit** — different from the main product's primary benefit, but reinforcing the use of the main product
2. **Elevate the experience** of the main product — help the buyer extract maximum value from the primary deliverable
3. **Be specific and described** — not "bonus video" but a named deliverable with described content and stated outcome
4. **Have a stated value** — anchored in market price for similar deliverables, so the stack mechanics work (§3.3 Pattern A)
5. **Have its own benefit narrative** — bonuses are mini-products within the offer; each needs its own bullet list of what it is, why it matters, what the buyer gains from it

**The "surprise gift" test**

The Strategist asks: *"If you wanted to give a surprising gift to someone who bought this product, what would you include?"*

The answer to that question is the bonus.

**Bonus archetypes (proven patterns)**

| Bonus type | Why it works | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| **Educational content** (book, magalog, report, mini-course) | "Info-first" model — educates the buyer on the category, increases consumption of the main product | When the buyer needs context to use the main product effectively |
| **In-depth course / video deep-dive** | Adds new knowledge layer beyond the main product | When the main product is broad and a sub-topic deserves expansion |
| **Community access** (forum, private group, networking) | Completely decoupled from the product's primary benefit; offers a different benefit (peer connection) | When the avatar has identity / belonging needs |
| **Live webinar with frontman** | Personal access to the expert; deep-dive on one specific problem | Books / front-end products / lead-magnets |
| **Templates / cheat sheets / Excel sheets / infographics** | Practical implementation aids decoupled from theory | When the main product is informational and execution is the user's pain |
| **Bonus with immediate gratification** | Combats buyer's remorse when the main product needs to ship | Physical products / cohort-based programs / scheduled deliverables |

**Bonus presentation — the rule**

A bonus is not "listed". A bonus is **sold**.

For each bonus, the copy must include:

1. **Name** (a real name — see `naming.md`; "the Atelier Module" beats "Bonus #3")
2. **Description** — what physical form it takes (video, doc, group invite, kit)
3. **Why it matters** — how it elevates the buyer's experience of the main product
4. **Future benefits** — what the buyer will be able to do, feel, or become after consuming this bonus

A bonus listed without these four elements is a wasted asset.

**The bonus consumption rule**

If the buyer doesn't consume the bonuses, the upsell pipeline collapses. Consumed bonuses → educated buyer → ready for the next purchase. Unconsumed bonuses → buyer doesn't activate → upsell fails.

Bonuses that work best are: high-perceived-value AND high-consumption-probability. Sometimes a smaller bonus with 80% consumption rate beats a bigger bonus with 10% consumption rate.

**Immediate gratification bonus**

Especially important when the main product has to ship or cohort-launch later. The buyer experiences "purchase regret" in the interval between payment and delivery. A digital bonus that's accessible immediately fills that interval.

Pattern: *"While your kit ships, today you also get instant access to [bonus name] — start using it tonight."*

### 3.5 Risk inversion

The risk inversion component does more than offer a guarantee. It addresses the buyer's full anxiety map:

**The buyer's fears**

| Fear | What it is |
|---|---|
| Losing money | The price is gone if the product disappoints |
| Losing face | Being criticized by peers for the purchase decision |
| Losing time | Wasted weeks/months on a wrong choice (in business: missing a season, losing market position to a competitor) |
| "Scientology effect" | Entering a high-pressure funnel of constant sales calls and emails |
| Fear of effort | Discovering the product requires work the buyer wasn't ready for |
| Insufficient results | The product works partially but not enough to justify the cost |
| Disappointment | Personal disappointment in the brand or the experience |

Risk inversion targets these systematically. The guarantee addresses "losing money"; social proof and credibility elements address "losing face"; track record addresses "losing time"; transparent process descriptions address the "Scientology effect" and "fear of effort"; case studies of similar-profile buyers address "insufficient results"; brand authenticity markers address "disappointment".

**Risk inversion = guarantee + proof + transparency + alignment markers.**

Pure guarantee without the other elements addresses only one fear of seven.

#### 3.5.1 Strong guarantees — 3 rules

A guarantee is strong if it satisfies all three rules:

**Rule 1 — Competitors read it and think "this is crazy"**

If a competitor in the category could comfortably offer the same guarantee, it isn't strong enough. A strong guarantee provokes envy or anxiety in competitors — it would force them to match it at margin cost they can't sustain.

**Rule 2 — The guarantee is described at length, not stamped**

A badge ("100% Money-Back Guarantee!") is not a guarantee. A guarantee is a section of copy that describes:

- What the guarantee covers
- How it is invoked (process)
- What the buyer gets back
- What the buyer keeps (bonuses, knowledge, side-products)
- Why the brand offers this guarantee (the underlying confidence reason)

This section usually runs 100-300 words. Badge alone underdelivers because the buyer doesn't believe it.

**Rule 3 — The guarantee has a NAME**

A named guarantee is memorable, distinct from the generic "30-day money-back" of every competitor, and ownable. Naming techniques from `naming.md` apply.

Pattern examples (illustrative, with technique noted):

- *"The Satisfied-or-Twice-Refunded Guarantee"* (Technique 0 descriptive + Technique 5 contrarian — the doubled refund is the contrarian frame)
- *"The Lawnmower Guarantee"* — even if you return the product damaged beyond use, refund stands. (Technique 1.7 vivid metaphor — the lawnmower image makes the guarantee unforgettable)
- *"The Mechanism Guarantee"* — refund tied specifically to whether the UM produced the stated outcome. (Anchored to `unique-mechanism.md` — see belief #26)

#### 3.5.2 The 10 types of guarantees

| # | Type | Pattern | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Conditional** | Customer must complete an action (use the product, follow the protocol) before invoking the guarantee | Programs/courses where execution matters; reduces refund abuse |
| 2 | **Unconditional** | One-click refund, no questions, no action required | Low-friction front-end products; trust-building |
| 3 | **Mixed** | Unconditional refund + additional reward for invoking with feedback | High-value memberships; collecting market feedback |
| 4 | **Pay back MORE than you spent** | Refund + bonus payment back to the buyer | High-confidence promises; bold positioning |
| 5 | **Pay back per result missed** | Refund per specific KPI not delivered | Services where deliverables are measurable (deadline missed → discount) |
| 6 | **Time-based (classic)** | 30 / 60 / 90 / 365-day window | Default for digital products; baseline |
| 7 | **Lifetime** | Refund window is permanent | Premium products where lifetime trust is the positioning |
| 8 | **Competitor / price-match** | Find a cheaper or better alternative, get the difference back | Commodity categories where price is the dominant variable |
| 9 | **Keep the bonuses** | If you refund the main product, you keep the bonuses | Events / cohort programs; reduces refund anxiety |
| 10 | **If it doesn't work, I pay you a trip** | Refund + experience compensation | When the brand can secure the experience at low cost-to-brand but high perceived-value (B2B leveraging corporate accounts) |

#### 3.5.3 Guarantee language — preeminence

Guarantees should use **positive language** (assuming the buyer will love the product, with the guarantee as a side-note) rather than **negative language** (anticipating disappointment).

**Negative pattern (WRONG)**:

> *"If you're not perfectly satisfied for any reason, message me and I'll refund you faster than [reference]."*

This pattern primes the buyer to look for reasons to be dissatisfied. It centers refund.

**Positive pattern (RIGHT)**:

> *"Either you become the happiest customer in the world, certain you've invested your time and money in the best product on the market — or one message from you is enough for me to refund you faster than [reference]."*

This pattern primes the buyer to expect satisfaction. The refund clause is a safety net, not the focus.

**Preeminence principle**: act like the family doctor who genuinely cares about the patient's long-term outcome. Recommend what's actually needed — not more, not less. Develop relational courage: be willing to tell the entrepreneur their offer needs work (risk losing them; if they understand, they stay forever).

### 3.6 Scarcity / urgency

Scarcity creates the reason the offer cannot wait. Without scarcity, every offer becomes "I'll come back to it" — and most buyers don't.

**Scarcity types**:

| Type | What it is | Anchoring requirement |
|---|---|---|
| **Time-based** | Offer expires on date/time | Must state WHY the date — launch period, end of campaign, cohort start, calendar event |
| **Stock-limited** | Limited units available | Must state WHY limited — production capacity, allocation, cohort size, capacity to deliver |
| **Cohort/seat-limited** | Limited seats in the program | Must state WHY the limit — bandwidth of frontman, room capacity, support ratio |
| **Tier-based** | Early-bird → standard → late pricing (the Kickstarter pattern) | Built into the structure; each tier has a stated quantity or date |
| **Reserved-segment** | Offer only available to specific list segment | Must state the segmentation logic — past customers, members, qualified leads |

**The anchoring rule**

Scarcity without a stated reason is **phantom scarcity** — the buyer doesn't believe it, and B4 (brand trustworthy) collapses. The Strategist must verify that every scarcity claim has a real, stateable reason.

Phantom scarcity examples to flag:

- "Only 5 spots left" with no production / capacity / cohort reason
- Countdown timer that resets when the buyer refreshes the page
- "Limited time offer" with no end date stated
- "While supplies last" without a reason supply is limited

Real scarcity examples:

- "Cohort of 50 — once filled, the next opens in 6 months" (cohort capacity)
- "Beta pricing while we collect 30 case studies — pricing changes Sept 1" (launch reason)
- "Only 200 units produced this quarter — shipping until inventory clears" (production capacity)
- "Founding-members rate available until the first 100 — then market price" (founding-cohort reason)

### 3.7 Call to action

The CTA is part of the offer, not separate from it. A weak CTA leaves a strong offer unaccepted.

**The 4 elements of a strong closing CTA**

1. **Future pacing** — describe the buyer's life N days/weeks/months after the purchase. Multi-sensory, specific, ideally tied to the avatar's stated dreams from `mass-desire.md`.

2. **Contrast technique** — white world (with the product) vs. black world (without). The contrast amplifies the cost of inaction.

3. **Spell out exactly what to do** — patient, clear, simple. "Click the button. Fill in your details. Choose your payment plan. Confirm. You'll receive an email with login details within 5 minutes."

4. **Why NOT acting is costly** — economically AND emotionally. The buyer must feel that NOT clicking has consequences (time loss, opportunity loss, continued discomfort).

**The closing template** (use with moderation — adapt to context, don't drop in raw):

```
This is what happened before [describe the situation]. Of course it
happened — [reasons the buyer has been disappointed by the category
in the past — pulls from chain-of-beliefs.md #8].

But this is what will happen now [future pacing] — thanks to the
[Unique Mechanism — from unique-mechanism.md] that will change
your experience of this kind of product forever.

I built the entire company around solving [problem]. My [Unique
Mechanism] is exactly what makes this simple.

If it didn't work, I wouldn't have been able to gather these
[proof elements: testimonials, publications, certifications — from
proof-elements.md] and I wouldn't be able to offer you this
[guarantee name — from §3.5.1].

Accept this proposal, and you'll never have to hear yourself say
[social fear — from avatar materials] again.
```

The template is illustrative. The actual close is calibrated to the avatar, the offer, and the format.

## 4. The 12 types of offers

Each offer falls into one or more of these structural patterns. The Strategist matches the pattern to the awareness level, the avatar's purchase capacity, and the brand's capacity to deliver.

| # | Type | Pattern | When to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Hard offer** | Pay everything upfront | High-trust audiences; recurring buyers; premium positioning |
| 2 | **Soft offer** | Order now, pay later (BNPL via Stripe/PayPal/Klarna) | E-commerce with hesitant buyers; markets where BNPL is established |
| 3 | **Installments / financing** | Spread payment over N months | High-ticket products; B2B equipment; longer-cycle services |
| 4 | **Membership / subscription** (with or without "puppy") | Recurring billing; "puppy" = trial period | Software / community / recurring-content products |
| 5 | **Launch offer with deadline** | One-time discount expiring on date | Product launches; founding-member cohorts |
| 6 | **Reserved offer (list segment)** | Specific list segment receives a unique offer | Upsells; customer-tier exclusives |
| 7 | **Until-stocks-last** | Limited quantity with stated production reason | Limited-batch physical products |
| 8 | **OTO (one-time offer)** | Offer presented at one specific funnel moment, never repeated | Upsells; cart-abandonment recovery |
| 9 | **Quantified offer (application/quiz-gated)** | Buyer applies; offer revealed after qualification | High-ticket B2B; consultations; cohort programs |
| 10 | **Bribe (with reason why)** | Free gift for attending/completing an action | Webinars, attendance incentives, engagement holds |
| 11 | **Upgrade with credit** | Past purchase credited toward higher-tier purchase | Service ladders (consultation → agency engagement; book → cohort) |
| 12 | **Contest / draw** | Drawing among attendees during the event/webinar | Live webinars to maximize end-of-event attendance |

### "Puppy" mechanic (Type 4 subtype)

The puppy trial: free trial of the membership, the buyer experiences attachment, conversion to paid follows. Named after the parental "no pets" rule that collapses once a puppy enters the home.

Effective for memberships where the experience of being inside the product is itself a strong conversion lever. Less effective for products whose value emerges only after weeks of use.

### "Order now, decide later" (a Type 1/2 hybrid)

A pattern that looks like a soft offer but is structurally a hard offer: the buyer's card is charged 7 days after purchase unless they cancel. The buyer mentally treats it as a trial but is already paid in.

Converts well because most buyers who would have refunded simply forget to cancel within 7 days. Use with care — high refund rates if the product disappoints; ethical considerations to weigh.

### Type 5 + Type 6 combined (cohort/founding-member pattern)

Common high-conversion stack: launch offer with deadline (Type 5) reserved to a specific list segment (Type 6). "Founding members only — pricing locks in after the first 100 or by [date]." Combines urgency + exclusivity + segmentation.

## 5. Reverse selling / takeaway selling — use with caution

"Takeaway selling" inverts the normal sales dynamic: the brand acts as if it doesn't need the buyer. Creates desire through scarcity-of-supply (or scarcity-of-attention from the brand). When it works, it elevates positioning. When it fails, it leaves money on the table.

### The 4 forms of reverse selling

1. **Takeaway** — "I won't sell this to you unless [qualification / condition]"
2. **Admission of weakness** — "Production is limited, I might not be able to ship right away" (often performative — works because it signals demand)
3. **What the product does NOT do** — explicit anti-features that disqualify buyers who wouldn't be a fit
4. **Who the product is NOT for** — explicit exclusion of segments who wouldn't benefit

### When takeaway selling works

- **Leader of category**: high-status brands can afford to be picky (the luxury-fashion model)
- **At-capacity operations**: sales force already overwhelmed; goal is to REDUCE lead volume and INCREASE qualification
- **Premium positioning campaigns**: where exclusivity is the central message

### When takeaway selling backfires

- **New brand**: no prior trust; market reads the reverse as desperation theater
- **Need for immediate revenue**: takeaway elongates the sales cycle; can't combine with short-runway business needs
- **Low awareness**: the avatar doesn't yet understand why this brand is special — takeaway reads as arrogance

### The underlying principle: **absence of need**

Need is the enemy of persuasion. A seller who appears desperate to close loses leverage. The takeaway technique is the surface expression; the underlying mindset is *"I have something valuable. If it fits you, we proceed. If not, no problem for either of us."*

This mindset shows up in copy even without explicit takeaway moves: a confident close, a willingness to disqualify, a refusal to discount. The Strategist surfaces this to the copywriter when the brand's current copy reads as needy.

## 6. Building offer value — the stacking patterns

How perceived value is constructed across the offer.

> Price-reframing boundary: the operational catalog of price-reframing techniques lives HERE (§6, plus the negotiating levers in §3.3); [persuasion-techniques](core/strategic-frameworks/persuasion-techniques.md) §3.4 covers WHEN to deploy Redefinition as a persuasion lever and points back to this section for the how.

### 6.1 The stack (Pattern A from §3.3)

The list price of the main product is held at face value. Bonuses are added, each with a stated market value. The buyer is told the total value (high), then offered the buyer-price (much lower).

```
Main product:              €497
Bonus 1 (named):           €197 value
Bonus 2 (named):           €297 value
Bonus 3 (named):           €197 value
Bonus 4 (named):           €497 value
─────────────────────────────────
Total stacked value:     €1,685
Your investment today:     €497
You save:                €1,188
```

**Rules for the stack**:

- Each bonus must have a real, defensible market value (not invented numbers)
- The total stack must be defensible — if a competitor sold the same bonuses separately, would they sell at these prices?
- The "you save" calculation must be honest math
- The stack works best in info-first businesses where bonuses are easy to value (courses, books, events, webinars)

### 6.2 Math for the buyer

The buyer doesn't want to do math. The copy does it for them.

Patterns:

- Per-day: €600/year → "less than €2/day"
- Per-use: €2,000 license → "if you use it 100 times, that's €20 per use"
- ROI projection: €5,000 investment → "if it returns even €50/month, breakeven in 100 months — and you'll do better than that"
- Loss-prevention: "Every month of [problem] costs you €X. The product is €Y. Math is obvious."

### 6.3 Dimensionalization

Anchoring the price against something the buyer already pays without thinking. Calibrated to the avatar's economic register.

- Mass market: "the price of a coffee a day" / "less than a Netflix subscription"
- Mid-tier: "less than a dinner out per month"
- High-tier: "the cost of a single tank of gas in your Range Rover"
- Ultra-premium: "what you spend on a bottle of [reference] when entertaining"

Wrong calibration sabotages the technique — anchoring a €30,000 program against "a coffee a day" produces 6,000 coffees per year and breaks credibility.

### 6.4 Loss-anchoring (cost of inaction)

The price is set against the cost of NOT acting. "Every month without [solution] = €X lost. The product is €Y. Inaction costs more than action within [N] months."

This pattern is especially powerful for business-tool offers and for health/relationship offers where the cost of the problem compounds over time.

## 7. Premium variant strategy

Most offers benefit from a tiered structure: Standard / Premium / VIP. Strategically deployed, this:

1. Captures the 20% of buyers who want the higher tier (rule of thumb: ~20% of any list will choose Premium when presented)
2. Anchors the Standard price (the Premium is the comparison anchor, making Standard feel reasonable)
3. Creates the downsell asset (if the buyer rejects Premium, Standard is the fallback)

### Tier construction principles

**Standard**: the core deliverable + basic bonuses. The minimum viable offer.

**Premium**: Standard + bonuses that the avatar values disproportionately — typically 1:1 access, priority support, exclusive content, certifications, faster delivery, accelerated outcomes.

**VIP / Ultra-Premium**: Premium + scarce, hard-to-replicate elements (limited cohort with frontman, in-person event, custom one-off deliverable, ongoing relationship). Usually capped at low quantity (5-20 buyers).

### The cinema-popcorn pattern (price manipulation)

The middle tier is priced just slightly below the top tier — making the top tier feel inevitable.

Classic example:

- Small popcorn: €2
- Medium popcorn: €7
- Large popcorn: €8

Most buyers take Large. The medium exists to sell the large.

Direct-response equivalent in supplements:

- 1-month supply: €50
- 3-month supply: €149 (€49.67/month)
- 5-month supply + accessories: €149 (€29.80/month — same total as 3-month but more product)

The 3-month tier exists to make the 5-month tier feel obviously correct.

The Strategist verifies: is the tier math designed to point the buyer toward the desired tier? Or is the price ladder accidental?

### The configurator pattern

For high-ticket B2B (equipment, services, custom solutions): the offer is built via configurator. The buyer selects features; the price assembles. Used when:

- The product has real configurable variables
- The price range is wide enough that one-price-fits-all fails
- The brand has the operations to deliver custom configurations

The configurator's psychological function is the same as price decomposition: the buyer sees small additions, each justified, and ends at a total that feels assembled rather than dropped on them.

### Application/quiz-gated pricing (Type 9 offer)

For very high-ticket offers, the price is not stated upfront. The buyer applies via quiz/form, gets pre-qualified, then receives the offer. This:

- Reduces lead volume (only qualified buyers proceed)
- Justifies the price (the qualifying experience builds investment)
- Creates the impression of selection (the brand picks the buyer, not the other way around)

Requires real qualification — if every applicant gets the offer, the mechanic is performative and damages B4.

## 8. Critical evaluation mode — auditing the brand's existing offers

This is the Strategist's most common operating mode. The brand has an offer (or several) in `brands/<brand>/offers.md`. The Strategist's job is to examine the offer with critical spirit and identify where it can be improved.

### Evaluation flow

**Step 1 — Read the existing offer**

Read `brands/<brand>/offers.md` for the specific offer relevant to this funnel. Read all surrounding context (product details, pricing, guarantee terms, bonus list).

**Step 2 — Score each of the 7 components**

For each component (§2), score:

- **Present and strong** — the component is in the offer and well-executed
- **Present but weak** — the component is there but underdelivers
- **Present but generic** — the component exists in a category-default form that adds no differentiation
- **Missing** — the component is absent

**Step 3 — Run the component-specific audits**

| Component | Audit checks |
|---|---|
| **Product** | Differentiated? Fully described? Delivery method specified? |
| **Benefits** | Every promise tied to a specific deliverable? F/E/E mix appropriate to the product? |
| **Price + terms** | List price + offer price both stated? Payment options match the avatar's capacity? Returns/delivery mechanics clear? |
| **Bonuses** | Each bonus offers a complementary (not duplicate) benefit? Each has name, description, why-it-matters, future-benefits? Stated values defensible? Immediate-gratification bonus included if main product ships? |
| **Risk inversion** | Guarantee present? Passes the 3 rules (§3.5.1)? Named? Described at length, not stamped? Addresses the buyer's full fear map (§3.5)? |
| **Scarcity / urgency** | Real (anchored to reason) or phantom? If anchored — to what? |
| **CTA** | Future pacing? Contrast? Spell out next action? Cost of inaction stated? |

**Step 4 — Identify the dominant leak**

Most offers leak value through one or two specific components. The Strategist names the dominant leak explicitly. Common leaks:

- **Leftover bonus syndrome** (§3.4): the bonuses are afterthoughts; complementary-benefit principle violated
- **Naked price**: price stated without stack, dimensionalization, or anchoring
- **Phantom scarcity**: urgency claimed without a defensible reason
- **Generic guarantee**: "30-day money-back" without a name, length, or anchoring to the UM
- **Promise without element**: copy promises things that no deliverable in the offer actually fulfills
- **No risk inversion beyond the guarantee**: the guarantee exists but the broader fear-map (§3.5) isn't addressed

**Step 5 — Propose specific improvements**

For each leak, propose a specific, actionable improvement:

- "The current guarantee badge is generic. Recommended: name it (see `naming.md`), describe it across 150-200 words, anchor it to the Unique Mechanism (belief #26)."
- "The price is presented naked. Recommended: build the stack against the bonuses (Pattern A); add per-day dimensionalization for the avatar's economic register."
- "Bonus #2 ('Recording of last year's event') offers the same benefit as the main product, just repackaged. Recommended: replace with a complementary bonus — propose [X] given the avatar's needs."

**Step 6 — Surface "must fix before launch" vs. "nice to improve"**

Some leaks are **structural** — the campaign cannot ship until they're fixed (e.g., promise-without-element problem; no guarantee at all on a high-ticket offer; phantom scarcity that would damage B4). Other leaks are **optimizations** that can be deferred to v2.

The Strategist flags structural leaks as blocking and optimizations as non-blocking. The copywriter decides which non-blocking optimizations to address before launch.

**Step 7 — Present to the copywriter**

The Strategist presents the evaluation as a structured audit:

- Components scored
- Dominant leak named
- Specific improvements proposed (with priority — structural vs. optimization)
- Recommendation: ship-as-is / quick-fix-then-ship / pause-and-rebuild

**Step 8 — Copywriter selects which improvements to apply**

Copywriter-led principle. The copywriter may:

- Accept all proposed improvements
- Accept the structural ones and defer optimizations
- Reject specific proposals with reasons
- Request the Strategist to draft specific language for the improvements

### The Strategist's posture in evaluation mode

- **Honest**: name the weaknesses clearly, even when the brand owner is emotionally invested in the current offer
- **Specific**: never "consider strengthening the guarantee" — always "rename the guarantee using Technique 1.7 from `naming.md`; describe it across 150-200 words; anchor to belief #26"
- **Constructive**: every leak identified comes with a proposed fix
- **Calibrated to brand reality**: don't propose a 365-day guarantee if the brand operates on thin margins; don't propose a high-touch VIP tier if the frontman has no bandwidth
- **Copywriter-led on final choices**: the Strategist proposes; the copywriter decides what to ship

## 10. False Offer — the trap

Like the other traps in the False family (canonical list in [unique-mechanism](core/strategic-frameworks/unique-mechanism.md) §5), a False Offer has the shape of an offer without the substance.

### Symptoms

- **Naked price**: a price tag and a product. No bonuses, no guarantee, no urgency, no CTA structure. This isn't an offer; it's a listing.
- **Leftover bonuses**: bonuses given for the sake of having bonuses; duplicate-benefit content with no consumption strategy.
- **Phantom scarcity**: countdown timers without reasons; "limited spots" without limits.
- **Badge-only guarantee**: a logo that says "100% Money-Back Guarantee" with no description, no name, no length.
- **Unanchored promises**: copy makes promises the offer cannot fulfill.
- **Negative-language guarantee**: written from the perspective of refund-expected rather than satisfaction-expected.
- **Pitched price**: the price is named without the stack, dimensionalization, or anchoring work that justifies it.

### Why False Offers are worse than no offer at all

The buyer's pattern recognition for "fake direct response" is well-trained in 2025. A False Offer triggers the same defensive reflex as a fabricated proof or a hollow acronym: B4 (brand trustworthy) collapses on contact.

### How to avoid

- Run the 7-component audit (§8) on every offer before campaign launch
- Reject any component scored "Present but generic" without an improvement plan
- Never ship a campaign with an offer that has a structural leak (§8 Step 6)
- If the brand resists offer improvements, the Strategist surfaces the risk explicitly to the copywriter; the copywriter decides whether to proceed with awareness of the weakness

### Cross-reference to the False family

The canonical list of the False traps lives in [unique-mechanism](core/strategic-frameworks/unique-mechanism.md) §5 — this file does not re-list it. False Offer (this section) is the offer member of that family: shape without leverage. All traps in the family share the same failure mode — surface without underlying substance — and the defense in each case is the criteria checklist + the audit, run rigorously and without shortcuts.

## 11. Output into the funnel brief

The offer block is **mandatory**. Every funnel ships with an offer; the brief documents which components are present, which are improved, and where leaks (if any) were accepted with awareness.

### Format — pointer

Fill the brief's Offer block — §3.6 of [funnel-brief](core/strategic-frameworks/funnel-brief.md), which owns the canonical block format. Do NOT redefine the block here.

Fields this file feeds into §3.6, component by component:

- **Mode of work** — Construction vs Evaluation (§9 Step 1)
- **Component 1 — Product / service** — description, delivery, differentiation (§3.1)
- **Component 2 — Benefits** — F / Ex / Em lists, each tied to an offer element (§3.2)
- **Component 3 — Price + terms** — list price, offer price, payment options, leak check (§3.3)
- **Component 4 — Bonuses** — name, description, why-it-matters, future benefit, stated value, immediate access, total stacked value (§3.4)
- **Component 5 — Risk inversion** — guarantee name, type, anchoring, 3-rules check, additional fear-map elements (§3.5)
- **Component 6 — Scarcity / urgency** — type, the actual limit, anchored reason, verifiability (§3.6)
- **Component 7 — Call to action** — future pacing, contrast, exact-action steps, cost of inaction (§3.7)
- **Offer type** — primary + hybrid types (§4)
- **Tier structure** — Standard / Premium / VIP contents (§7)
- **Audit / leak summary** (Evaluation mode) — dominant leak, improvements applied/deferred, accepted weaknesses (§8)

Note — the brief's §3.6 block ALSO requires these fields, easy to miss because they live deep in this file's sections:

- **Reason for discount** — mandatory whenever a real discount is offered (§3.3 Pattern B)
- **Guarantee duration** — N days / Lifetime / Other (§3.5.2)
- **Description verbatim** — the full 100-300-word guarantee paragraph as it will appear in copy (§3.5.1 Rule 2)
- **Phantom-scarcity check** — Pass / Fail (§3.6 anchoring rule)
- **CTA verbatim suggestion** — button text + reassurance copy (§3.7)
- **Tier strategy** — which tier is primary, which feeds upsell/high-touch (§7)

### Worked example (evaluation-mode excerpt)

> Non-normative content illustration. The canonical block format is funnel-brief §3.6; this example shows the depth of an evaluation-mode audit, not the format to copy.

```
OFFER

  Mode of work: Evaluation (existing offer audited)

  ─── Components (scored) ───
    Product:         Strong — differentiated, well-described
    Benefits:        Weak — promises "transformation" without anchoring to a specific element
    Price + terms:   Weak — €497 stated naked; no stack, no dimensionalization, no installment option
    Bonuses:         Generic — 2 bonuses but both are "recordings" duplicating main-product benefit
    Risk inversion:  Weak — "30-day money-back" badge only; no name, no description, no length
    Scarcity:        Phantom — "limited spots" with no stated reason
    CTA:             Generic — "Click here to purchase"

  Dominant leak: Risk inversion + Bonuses (both leaking simultaneously is the failure pattern)

  Proposed improvements (priority: STRUCTURAL):
    1. Bonuses — replace 2 "recordings" with 2 complementary bonuses:
       - Bonus A: "The 30-Day Implementation Playbook" — daily checklists tied to the main product (immediate gratification, stated value €197)
       - Bonus B: "Private cohort channel access — 12 weeks" — community/networking decoupled from product (stated value €297)
    2. Risk inversion — name the guarantee, describe across ~200 words, anchor to UM (belief #26):
       - Proposed name: "The Mechanism Guarantee" (via naming.md Technique 0 + UM anchor)
       - Anchor: if [UM] does not produce [stated outcome] within [timeframe], full refund + keep bonuses
       - 3-rules check after rewrite: yes / yes / yes
    3. Scarcity — anchor to a real reason or remove:
       - Recommended: tie to cohort capacity (20 spots; next cohort 6 months later); else remove the "limited spots" claim entirely
    4. Price + terms — build stack:
       - With Bonus A (€197) + Bonus B (€297) + main product (€497) → total stacked value €991
       - Today price €497 → "you save €494"
       - Add 3-month installment option (€177/mo)
       - Add per-day dimensionalization: €1.36/day across the program duration

  Proposed improvements (priority: OPTIMIZATION):
    5. Benefits — anchor each promise to a specific deliverable; split F/E/E
    6. CTA — add future pacing + cost-of-inaction
    7. Tier structure — consider Premium tier (€997) with 1:1 onboarding call (captures the 20% premium-buyer slice)

  Recommendation: pause-and-fix-structural-then-ship (improvements 1-4 are blocking; 5-7 can be deferred to v2)

  Strategist note for copywriter: the existing offer cannot ship as-is — risk inversion + bonuses leaks are structural. Even the best copy on top of this offer will underperform. Suggest 3-5 days to implement the structural fixes before writing the campaign.
```
