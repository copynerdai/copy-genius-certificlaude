# Mass Desire — Selecting the engine of every funnel

> Foundational strategic framework. Identifies WHICH mass desire the funnel channels and WHICH product performance satisfies it. Output: a single (desire ↔ performance) pair that anchors the entire funnel brief.
>
> **Consumer contract**
> Read by: the **Copy Genius Strategist** — at: Phase 1 (Discovery), Step 4 (Mass Desire) — BEFORE awareness mapping (Step 5) and sophistication assessment (Step 6), since those build on the desire chosen here.
> Output: fills funnel-brief block §3.2 (Mass Desire) — [funnel-brief](core/strategic-frameworks/funnel-brief.md) owns the block format; this file only explains how to derive the content.
> Load-bearing sections (always read): §3 (the three dimensions + scoring), §4 (product analysis), §5 (the 7-step operational sequence), §7 (data dependency map). Reference (on demand): §2 (the two categories of desire forces), §6 (diagnostic signals), §8 (brief output sketch), §9 (cross-references).

---

## 1. Core principle

Copy doesn't create desire. It channels desire that already exists in the market.

A mass desire is the **public spread of a private want**: enough individuals privately wanting the same thing that a profitable market exists to serve them.

The copywriter cannot create mass desire — only governments, social movements, technological shifts, and educational currents can do that. The copywriter's job is to:

1. **Detect** mass desires already at work
2. **Choose** the right one for THIS product, THIS market, RIGHT NOW
3. **Match** it to the product performance that satisfies it most powerfully
4. **Channel** all of this into a single message that turns latent desire into action

The reward for doing this right is the **Amplification Effect**: when an ad taps existing desire, $1 of advertising can generate $50, $100 or more in sales (illustrative, not a rule). When an ad tries to *create* desire, it produces at best $1 in sales for $1 spent — that's education, not advertising, and no advertiser can afford it.

This principle and the analytical framework that follows are part of the public copywriting canon (Eugene Schwartz).

## 2. The two categories of mass desire forces

Mass desires emerge from two distinct sources. The Strategist categorizes each candidate desire under one of these to understand its origin and behavior.

### 1. Permanent forces — desires that never fade

**Mass instinct**
Desires rooted in human biology and social drives. They don't change across decades or centuries.

Examples of universal mass instincts:
- The instinct to be physically attractive
- The instinct to be perceived as competent, strong, capable
- The instinct to be safe (physically, financially, socially)
- The instinct to belong to a group
- The instinct to be admired, respected, recognized
- The instinct to leave a legacy / matter to others
- The instinct to feel healthy and energetic
- The instinct to protect loved ones
- The instinct to be free from suffering, pain, fear
- The instinct to provide for one's family

The Strategist's challenge with mass instincts: the desire is so obvious that everyone in the market is already targeting it. The job becomes **how to make this product's claim fresh and believable again** — typically through a unique mechanism, a unique angle, or a new proof.

**Mass technological problem**
Persistent unsolved problems that customers keep trying to solve. Each new solution gets purchased; each failure pushes the customer to try the next.

Examples of mass technological problems:
- Aging skin, hair loss
- Joint and back pain
- Insomnia / poor sleep quality
- Energy slumps and chronic fatigue
- Slow internet / poor connectivity
- Spam and unwanted notifications
- Long laundry / cleaning times
- Stubborn stains, odors, household nuisances
- Recurring software glitches or device limitations

The desire repeats: until the problem is genuinely solved, the customer buys and tries again. The Strategist's challenge: offer the same relief claim, but **in a new way** that breaks through saturation.

### 2. Forces of change — desires that emerge and evolve

**Trends**
Mass shifts in what people want — driven by culture, economics, technology, fashion. Trends have a beginning, a peak, and a reversal.

Examples of recent trend-driven desires:
- The shift to AI-assisted work
- The shift to digital wellness / screen-time control
- The shift to plant-based eating
- The shift to remote work and asynchronous collaboration
- The shift to clean ingredients / no-additives food
- The shift to short-form video content
- The shift to subscription-based access over ownership

The Strategist's challenge with trends: **timing**. Be there as it rises (early enough to dominate, late enough that the market exists), recognize the peak, see the reversal coming.

**Mass education**
Top-down propagation of new aspirations or values: from media figures, public personalities, opinion leaders, advertising itself, group pressure, community products → into the broad public.

Examples of education-driven desires:
- A new diet protocol enters mainstream after public figures adopt it
- A previously niche supplement enters mass awareness through influencer content
- A new fashion category emerges from celebrity endorsement
- A new workplace practice (4-day week, async work) spreads via thought leaders

The Strategist's challenge with mass education: **when has the shift become statistically significant?** When have enough people made the change for the market to be profitable to address?

## 3. The three dimensions of every desire

Once candidate desires are identified, each is scored on three orthogonal dimensions. The Strategist selects the desire that maximizes all three together.

### Dimension 1 — Urgency / Intensity

How acute is this desire RIGHT NOW for the prospect?

- A constant arthritic pain has urgency 5 (never stops; sufferer would pay heavily for relief now).
- A minor headache has urgency 2 (annoying but tolerable).
- A vague desire to "be more productive someday" has urgency 1.

Diagnostic questions:
- Does the prospect actively seek a solution right now, or might they wait years?
- Is the discomfort/desire felt daily, or only occasionally?
- Does the prospect lose sleep over this?
- Would they pay tomorrow what they wouldn't pay today?

### Dimension 2 — Staying power / Repetition

Is this desire satisfied once and gone, or does it keep returning?

- Raw hunger has staying power 5 (must be satisfied every day).
- Buying a wedding ring has staying power 1 (one-time purchase, rarely revisited).
- A craving for status / recognition has staying power 4 (renewed continuously, never fully sated).

Diagnostic questions:
- Will the prospect want this AGAIN next month, next year?
- Does the satisfaction wear off, or does it permanently solve the desire?
- Is this a one-shot purchase or a continuity opportunity?

High staying power = higher lifetime value, easier scaling of campaigns, better unit economics.

### Dimension 3 — Scope / Reach

How many people share this desire at this price point?

- The desire to lose weight has scope = millions (worldwide, across demographics).
- The desire for fine antique restoration has scope = few thousand (niche).
- The desire to manage a specific chronic condition has scope = limited but acute.

Diagnostic questions:
- How many people fit the avatar?
- Of those, how many would pay the price point being considered?
- What's the natural ceiling for this market?

Scope alone doesn't decide — a small market with high urgency and high staying power often beats a vast market that's lukewarm and one-shot. But scope sets the maximum scale of the funnel.

### Scoring rubric

For each candidate desire, the Strategist assigns 1-5 on each dimension and notes the rationale:

| Desire candidate | Urgency | Staying | Scope | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Desire A | 5 | 4 | 4 | Strong all around |
| Desire B | 3 | 5 | 5 | Less urgent but wider |
| Desire C | 5 | 2 | 3 | Acute but one-shot |

The Strategist doesn't just sum — it **weights** based on the funnel's strategic intent (acquisition vs LTV vs market size). Default weighting profiles:

- **Acquisition funnel** → weight **Urgency** highest (the desire must convert cold traffic now)
- **LTV / backend funnel** → weight **Staying power** highest (the desire must keep returning across the customer lifetime)
- **Niche domination** → weight **Scope** highest (the desire must cover enough of the addressable niche to own it)

The Strategist proposes the profile matching the funnel's intent; **the copywriter confirms the weighting**. The funnel brief should make the weighting explicit.

## 4. Product analysis — physical vs functional

After selecting the dominant desire, the Strategist analyzes the product to find the performance that satisfies that desire.

### The distinction

Every product is two products:

- **Physical product** — the material substrate: ingredients, components, code, materials, format, modules, hours of content, packaging, specifications.
- **Functional product** — the product in action: the series of benefits the user experiences, the transformations the product enables.

**Physical does NOT sell. Functional sells.**

Nobody buys "steel" or "vitamin B12" or "8 modules". They buy what those things DO for them: safety, energy, mastery, freedom from a problem, becoming a different version of themselves.

### Use of the physical (always subordinate to the functional)

The physical product has five legitimate uses in copy. It never carries the headline alone — it supports the functional claim:

1. **Justify the price** — the longer the car / the more vitamins in the formula / the more hours of content → the more it can credibly cost.
2. **Document the quality** — specific specifications (weight, purity, certification, manufacturing process) make the claim more believable.
3. **Assure continuity** — the physical proves the benefit will last (ceramic mufflers = no repair bills; archival-grade paper = pass the book to your children).
4. **Sharpen the mental picture** — concrete physical details make the promise vivid ("every metal part shielded from every other metal part by a protective coat of rubber" — that's why the car is silent).
5. **Provide a fresh basis for believability** — when the claim is saturated, a NEW physical detail (new ingredient, new manufacturing process, new component) re-proves the old promise.

The physical NEVER goes in the headline alone. It only supports the functional claim in the body.

### The performance inventory

The Strategist lists EVERY performance the product delivers. For a typical product, this is 5-15 distinct performances.

Example for an automobile (illustrative, used in the public canon):
- Transportation (carry people, luggage, animals from place to place)
- Dependability (no breakdowns, low repair frequency, no embarrassment)
- Economy (gas savings, low insurance, high resale)
- Power (acceleration, top speed, towing, dominance on the highway)
- Recognition (status, admiration, "the new car smell", neighbor envy)
- Value (durability per dollar, long mileage life)
- Novelty (latest technology, latest aesthetics, pioneer feeling)
- Identity expression (the type of person who drives this)

For each performance, the Strategist asks: **which mass desire does this performance satisfy?**

### Grouping performances by mass desire satisfied

| Performance | Mass desire satisfied |
|---|---|
| Power | Instinct to dominate / be capable |
| Recognition | Instinct for status / approval / belonging |
| Economy | Instinct for safety / financial security |
| Dependability | Instinct for safety / freedom from worry |
| Novelty | Instinct to stand out / be a pioneer |

This grouping makes the next step trivial: the desire chosen in the previous phase already points to the performance to feature.

### Selecting THE ONE performance

The Strategist picks the ONE performance that:

1. Satisfies the desire selected in the previous step
2. Is genuinely DELIVERABLE — the product really does this; no overpromising
3. Is DIFFERENT from how competitors frame the same desire, where possible — links to sophistication stage assessment

This becomes the **dominant claim** of the funnel.

## 5. The operational sequence (7 steps)

The Strategist runs this sequence during Phase 1 of brief building.

### Step 1 — Inventory candidate desires

Pull from existing brand knowledge:

- `brands/<brand>/avatars/<segment>.md` → pains, desires, Maslow drivers, social mirror entries
- `brands/<brand>/research/psychographic.md` → market-level desires from research
- `brands/<brand>/research/unified-brief.md` → main promises that resonate; objections (objections often reveal blocked desires)
- `brands/<brand>/brand.md` → what the brand stands for / against (hints at desires it can serve credibly)

Expect 5-10 candidate desires to emerge. Each phrasable as "the desire to <verb> <object>" or "the desire to BE <adjective>".

If the existing knowledge yields fewer than 5 strong candidates, the Strategist asks the copywriter:
> "I've pulled these candidate desires from the brand files. What other desires are alive in this market that aren't captured here?"

### Step 2 — Score each candidate on the three dimensions

For each candidate, assign Urgency / Staying power / Scope (1-5) with rationale. Sources:

- Evidence from `research/` files
- Competitive context from `research/competitor-research.md`
- Direct input from the copywriter where signals are thin

If a dimension is genuinely unknown, the Strategist asks the copywriter rather than guessing.

### Step 3 — Select THE ONE dominant desire

The chosen desire must:

- Score high (≥ 4) on at least two of three dimensions
- Be a desire the product can genuinely satisfy (no hollow promises)
- Align with the copywriter's intuition of strongest pull for this audience right now

If multiple desires tie or score close, the Strategist surfaces the tie to the copywriter and asks for the tiebreaking criterion (acquisition focus vs LTV focus vs differentiation from competitors).

**Fallback — no candidate reaches the threshold.** If no desire scores ≥ 4 on at least two of the three dimensions, do NOT force a selection. This is a red signal: either the market data is too thin, the avatar work hasn't gone deep enough, or the market itself isn't ready. The Strategist:

1. Stops Step 3 and reports the full scoring table to the copywriter
2. Recommends one path forward: targeted deep research on the gap, avatar refinement, or market re-evaluation
3. Does NOT proceed to Step 4 on weak ground — the brief built on a tepid desire will underperform regardless of what comes after

### Step 4 — List the product's performances

From `brands/<brand>/products.md`, the Strategist extracts (for the product or bundle the funnel sells):

- All "Key selling points" → headline-grade performances
- All "Benefits" → supporting/secondary performances
- "Key features / ingredients / differentiators" → physical (used to support, never to lead)

**If the funnel's offer is a bundle (multi-product)**, the Strategist:

- Reads `brands/<brand>/offers.md` for the offer's "Products in this offer" list
- Lists performances from EACH product in the bundle (front-end, add-ons, bonus content)
- Treats **bundle-level synergies** as legitimate performance candidates in their own right — e.g., "complete system end-to-end" as a performance distinct from any single component, or "everything you need in one place" as a desire-satisfier the individual products can't claim alone
- The dominant performance selected in Step 6 can come from any single product in the bundle, OR from the bundle's synergy itself

If `products.md` is thin or doesn't capture the actual performances, the Strategist asks:
> "What does this product actually DO for the buyer? Walk me through the transformations it enables, not the features it contains."

### Step 5 — Group performances by mass desire satisfied

Build the grouping table. Each performance maps to one (or more) mass desires it satisfies.

### Step 6 — Select THE ONE performance

Pick the performance that:

- Maps directly to the desire chosen in Step 3
- Is the product's strongest delivery of that desire
- Is differentiated where possible (informs the sophistication stage discussion later)

### Step 7 — Document the (desire ↔ performance) pair in the brief

The brief contains an explicit anchor block:

```
SELECTED MASS DESIRE
  Desire: "[clear phrasing]"
  Category: [Permanent: Mass Instinct / Mass Technological Problem]
            [or Force of Change: Trend / Mass Education]
  Scoring: Urgency X/5 | Staying power X/5 | Scope X/5
  Source signals: [file references — avatar X, research section Y, ...]

SELECTED PRODUCT PERFORMANCE
  Performance: "[clear phrasing]"
  From product: [slug from products.md]
  Match rationale: [1-2 lines: why THIS performance satisfies THIS desire]

FUNNEL ENGINE STATEMENT
  This funnel speaks to [desire] by promising [performance].
```

This pair becomes the **engine of the funnel**. Awareness mapping, sophistication assessment, chain of beliefs, big idea (if used), and proof strategy all build on this.

## 6. Diagnostic signals (recognize and correct common errors)

**Signal: the chosen desire feels generic ("more money", "be healthier")**
→ Too abstract. Push for specificity. WHICH version? More money to leave a job? More money to retire early? More money to silence a critical parent? The specific framing IS the desire.

**Signal: the chosen performance is a physical feature**
→ Wrong layer. Re-extract: what does that feature DO for the buyer? The feature is support, not headline.

**Signal: desire and performance don't map cleanly to each other**
→ The brief will be incoherent. Either change the desire to one this product genuinely satisfies, or change the performance to one that actually serves this desire.

**Signal: the brief lists multiple co-equal desires**
→ Forbidden. Only ONE desire predominates. Multiple "main" desires = no main desire = no headline. The Strategist forces a choice.

**Signal: the chosen desire passes the dimensions test but the copywriter is unconvinced**
→ Stop. Don't override copywriter intuition with rubric alone. The rubric is a guide, not an oracle. Ask the copywriter what doesn't sit right, integrate it, re-score.

**Signal: the product hasn't been properly analyzed for performances; the inventory is thin**
→ Products.md is incomplete. Update products.md FIRST (with the copywriter's help) — don't try to brief over a thin product profile.

**Signal: copywriter pushes back on selecting just one desire**
→ Common. Acknowledge the others as secondary (they appear in body copy, in proof points, in follow-up emails) — but ONE leads the headline, and that ONE determines the funnel's tone.

## 7. Where data comes from (data dependency map)

| Step | Reads from | Asks copywriter if missing |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Inventory desires | `avatars/<segment>.md`, `research/psychographic.md`, `research/unified-brief.md`, `brand.md` | "What other desires are alive in this market that aren't captured in the files?" |
| 2. Score 3 dimensions | `research/unified-brief.md`, `research/competitor-research.md`, copywriter intuition | "Help me weigh urgency for desire X — how acute is it for the typical prospect today?" |
| 3. Select desire | All above + copywriter judgment | "Two desires tie. Which matters more for THIS funnel — broader acquisition or stronger conversion?" |
| 4. List performances | `products.md` (selling points, benefits, features) | "Are there performances you'd add that aren't in products.md?" |
| 5. Group performances | Step 4 + Step 1 | — |
| 6. Select performance | Step 5 + Step 3 | — |
| 7. Document in brief | All above | — |

The Strategist NEVER skips a step due to missing data. If data is missing, it asks. The copywriter answers. The work proceeds.

## 8. Outputs into the funnel brief

After this analysis, the Strategist writes into the brief:

1. **The selected mass desire** — clearly stated, with 3-dimension scoring and category (Permanent / of Change)
2. **The selected product performance** — clearly stated, linked to the desire with rationale
3. **Candidate desires considered but not chosen** — kept for reference; may resurface in body copy, follow-up emails, or alternative funnel variants
4. **Supporting physical product details** — the relevant items from products.md (key features / ingredients / differentiators) tagged with their function (justify price / document quality / assure continuity / sharpen picture / fresh believability) — used later by specialists

This output feeds directly into:

- **Awareness mapping** — where does the prospect sit relative to this specific desire and product?
- **Sophistication assessment** — how worn out is THIS desire claim in THIS market?
- **Chain of beliefs** — what must the prospect believe to act on this desire?
- **Big idea** (if applicable) — the unifying vehicle through which the desire is expressed
- **Proof strategy** — what evidence makes the desire+performance claim believable?
