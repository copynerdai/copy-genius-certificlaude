## 1. Core principle

A **Unique Mechanism** is a proprietary system through which a product delivers its effect with **at least one relevant difference** from other products in the same category.

A real UM must do two things simultaneously:

1. **Differentiate** the product from category alternatives (otherwise the prospect can comparison-shop on price)
2. **Make the product preferable** for the prospect's specific situation (differentiation without preference is mere distinction)

A UM is real when behind its name there is a **Reason Why** — a substantive answer to "why does this make the product work better for THIS prospect?". Without a Reason Why, what looks like a UM is fabricated: a name appended to nothing. This trap has its own section (5).

### The UM is a two-part logical connection (UMP + UMS)

A Unique Mechanism is not a single statement — it's a **two-part logical pair**:

- **UMP** (Unique Mechanism of the Problem) — explains WHY past solutions failed for the prospect. The "knowledge gap" they had. The 1% of information they were missing.
- **UMS** (Unique Mechanism of the Solution) — explains WHY this solution will succeed where others failed. How the product specifically addresses the UMP.

The persuasive force of a UM comes from the logical bridge between these two parts:

> "You've been failing because of [UMP]. This product succeeds because of [UMS], which directly addresses [UMP]."

A UM without an explicit UMP floats in a vacuum (the prospect doesn't see why this is different from what they've tried). A UMP without a UMS leaves the prospect frustrated but without a path forward. Both parts are needed; both must logically connect.

This UMP/UMS structure is the **architecture** of every real UM. The "3 types" introduced in section 3 are about WHERE the UM comes from (real / hidden / artificial). The UMP/UMS pair is HOW the UM is structured — it applies regardless of type. Articulation templates are in section 7.

### Relationship between UM and sophistication

The UM is not always needed. Whether it is needed — and how it should be communicated — is determined by the **market sophistication stage** (see section 2).

- At sophistication Stages 1-2, a UM is NOT needed; the work is on the promise itself.
- At Stage 3, the UM enters as the new center of the argument.
- At Stage 4, the UM is elaborated and a counter-mechanism dynamic emerges.
- At Stage 5, the field is exhausted and identification replaces mechanism.

The two concepts are inseparable, which is why they share one file.

### USP exception (brief note)

If a brand owns a true Unique Selling Proposition — a benefit no competitor can offer because of legal exclusivity (patent, monopoly, regulatory moat) — then the exclusivity itself functions as the differentiation. The Strategist doesn't fabricate a UM in that case: the exclusivity IS the answer to "why this product?". This rare situation is probed explicitly at question 9 of the interrogation (section 4 — the legal/patent exclusivity probe). The rest of this file addresses the much more common case where a UM must be discovered or constructed.

## 2. Market sophistication — the 5 stages

Sophistication measures how many promises the prospect has already heard in this category. As the market matures, promises wear out; what worked in early stages stops working as the market saturates.

This framework is part of the public copywriting canon (Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising*, Chapter 3).

**Selecting the sophistication stage is a copywriter-led decision.** The Strategist gathers signals from the brand wiki (competitor messaging style, category history, market awareness data) and presents the candidate stage; the copywriter confirms or overrides. See section 10 for the operational sequence.

### What to do at each stage (at a glance)

```
Stage 1 — FIRST in market
  Strategy: PURE PROMISE (claim alone). No UM.

Stage 2 — Promise becomes ENLARGED
  Strategy: EXPANDED PROMISE. Add specificity, scale, vividness. Still no UM.

Stage 3 — Promise saturated, UM ENTERS
  Strategy: UM is the center. Existing or Hidden UM preferred.

Stage 4 — Mechanism elaborated
  Strategy: UM ELABORATED. Refinement of the mechanism vs competitors.

Stage 5 — Field exhausted
  Strategy: IDENTIFICATION replaces mechanism. Transubstantiated UM + brand personality.
```

### Stage 1 — First in market

The brand is the first (or one of the very first) making this claim in this market. The prospect has never heard the basic claim before.

**What to do.** State the claim simply and directly. The pure promise alone moves the market. No mechanism needed, no elaborate verbalization — the claim itself is news.

**Diagnostic signals**:
- No competitors making similar claims
- No public conversation about the category
- The basic promise still feels fresh and arresting

### Stage 2 — Enlarged claim

Competitors have appeared and started making similar claims. Mere repetition no longer works. The market is the same, but the claim must intensify.

**What to do.** Enlarge the existing claim — more specific numbers, sharper outcomes, stronger guarantees, extended scope. Still working on the promise itself, not on a mechanism. The work is to outbid the basic claim by amplifying it.

**Diagnostic signals**:
- 2-5 competitors making similar basic claims
- The market is still responsive to claim intensification
- No competitor is yet leading with a proprietary mechanism

### Stage 3 — Unique Mechanism enters

The basic claims have been pushed to the limits of believability. The market is skeptical of bigger promises. A new device is needed to make the old claim feel fresh and credible: a Unique Mechanism. The headline shifts from "what the product does" to "how it works".

**What to do.** Introduce the UM. The mechanism becomes the new center of the argument. The claim becomes shorthand — the mechanism does the persuasive work.

**Diagnostic signals**:
- Multiple competitors making maximalist claims that no longer move the market
- Skepticism in the market ("I've heard this before, why should I believe it now?")
- The category is recognized, but conversion is dropping despite advertising spend
- The first brands using mechanism naming are starting to outperform pure-claim brands

### Stage 4 — Mechanism elaborated

Competitors have caught up and now have their own mechanisms. The first mechanism has been copied or matched. Differentiation moves to the proof and refinement layer.

**What to do.** Elaborate the UM. New refinement, additional certification, expanded proof, removal of former limitations of the mechanism itself. The Reason Why behind the UM gets sharpened.

**Diagnostic signals**:
- Multiple competitors with named mechanisms
- Mechanism naming is widespread in the category
- Differentiation now operates at the proof / certification / sub-segment fit layer

### Stage 5 — Field exhausted

The market has been saturated by claims, mechanisms, and counter-mechanisms. Prospects are tired of all of it. New mechanisms no longer get accepted — the prospect's pattern recognition kicks in and rejects them.

**What to do.** Shift from mechanism to identification. The Transubstantiated UM (artificial naming + storytelling — see section 3) becomes the path. The work moves from "what makes this product different" to "this product is for people like ME". Brand personality, identification copy, and sub-segment targeting take over.

**Diagnostic signals**:
- Multiple credible mechanisms in the market, all worn out
- Competitors leaving the category or shifting messaging to identification
- Prospects responding to lifestyle/identity messaging more than to feature messaging
- Mechanism names themselves are starting to trigger skepticism

## 3. The 3 types of Unique Mechanism (Stage 3+)

Once sophistication reaches Stage 3, a UM is needed. There are three operational types — each suited to a different situation.

### Type A — Existing UM (intrinsic)

The brand genuinely has a proprietary system: a unique formulation, a unique process, a unique combination of technologies, a unique methodology developed over time. The work is to **discover and name** what's already there.

**When to use**: when the 9-question interrogation (section 4) surfaces substantive answers — the brand really does things differently in ways the prospect cares about.

**Sophistication fit**: typical at Stage 3 (when a real UM enters the market for the first time) and Stage 4 (when the UM gets elaborated).

**Naming**: see §9 (naming requirement by UM type).

### Type B — Hidden UM (unspoken)

All competitors in the category use the same process or quality, but **none of them communicate it**. The first brand to articulate what everyone is doing claims the mechanism in the prospect's mind, even though the underlying reality isn't unique.

**When to use**: when the 9-question interrogation surfaces processes the brand uses that ARE shared with competitors, but the category as a whole has never made these processes visible to the prospect.

**Sophistication fit**: most powerful at Stage 3. A Hidden UM can essentially "freeze" the category at Stage 3 for the brand that articulates it first — for as long as competitors don't catch on.

**Naming**: see §9 (naming requirement by UM type).

### Type C — Transubstantiated UM (artificial)

The brand has no genuine unique difference. A UM is constructed via naming and storytelling. The product itself is a commodity; the marketing work creates the perception of uniqueness around it.

**When to use**: only when types A and B genuinely don't work — the 9-question interrogation produces no substantive answers, no hidden process is available to articulate. Type C is the last resort, not the default.

**Sophistication fit**: typical at Stage 5, when the market is exhausted of real mechanisms and only identification breaks through.

**Naming**: see §9 (naming requirement by UM type — obligatory for Type C).

**Warning**: transubstantiated UMs are easy to confuse with the **False Mechanism** (section 5). The distinguishing factor: a Type C UM has substantive Reason Why behind the name (a real angle, a real reframing of the category, a real sub-segment served). A False Mechanism has only the name and nothing behind it. Build Type C UMs only when the rest of the architecture (target sub-segment, distinctive angle, supporting proof) is solid.

## 4. The 9 questions to discover the UM (operational)

The Strategist runs a structured interrogation to discover the UM. The questions are asked to the brand owner (or, for self-developed brands, by the copywriter to themselves).

**Posture**: not a checklist. Each question opens a depth-dive. The Strategist asks "why" repeatedly until a substantive, concrete answer emerges. Vague answers are red flags, not stopping points.

**Timing realities**:
- Educated client (already marketing-savvy): 1.5-2 hours for the full interrogation
- Technical or unmarketed brand: 4+ hours, often spread across 2+ sessions
- Best practice: send the questions in advance, then meet — the brand owner pre-thinks the answers, the interrogation goes deeper

### The 9 questions

**1. What is your approach?**

- Surface intent: how the product/service works.
- Depth probe: sequence of steps, components, ingredients, methods. If the answer is generic ("we use the best ingredients", "we take care of every customer"), keep probing for specifics.

**2. How does your approach work?**

- Surface intent: the mechanism of operation.
- Depth probe: how does each step contribute to the result? What does each step DO? If "we use a special process", what makes the process special?

**3. Why does it work?**

- Surface intent: the causal mechanism behind the result.
- Depth probe: what is the scientific, mechanical, or behavioral reason this approach produces the outcome? If the brand owner can't answer, this is a major red flag — the brand may be running on tradition or luck rather than understanding.

**4. What makes it effective?**

- Surface intent: the effectiveness lever (distinct from "why it works").
- Depth probe: of all the things the approach does, which are the ones that produce the outcome? What are the routine parts vs. the high-leverage parts?

**5. Why these precise steps in this precise order?**

- Surface intent: test whether the sequence is deliberate or accidental.
- Depth probe: could two steps be swapped? Could a step be removed? If the brand owner says "we just do it this way", probe deeper — sometimes the sequence is genuinely ARBITRARY (no UM here), sometimes it's DELIBERATE for reasons the brand owner hasn't articulated (Hidden UM here).

**6. What have you added or removed in your process compared to competitors?**

- Surface intent: identify operational differences vs. the category.
- Depth probe: even if "we do the same as everyone", probe for small refinements, omissions, additions. The brand owner has often subconsciously done things differently — but only verbalizes it when asked directly. **This question most often surfaces the Hidden UM.**

**7. What benefit does your method bring to the client?**

- Surface intent: the outcome the client experiences.
- Depth probe: be specific. Numbers, time frames, comparisons. Reject vague "we make them happier" — push for measurable outcomes ("the average client sees X in Y time"). If the brand owner doesn't know in measurables, ask for customer stories — outcomes often emerge from the case histories.

**8. Why do you believe your method is particularly suited for THIS target client?**

- Surface intent: identify target alignment.
- Depth probe: why is this approach right for THIS demographic/psychographic, vs. other approaches that might be right for others? This question forces recognition that no method is universally best — the method's strength comes from its fit with a specific audience.

**9. In what aspect do you believe your method is better than others?**

- Surface intent: the dominant comparative claim.
- Depth probe: faster? Safer? More comfortable? More durable? Cheaper over time? More personalized? More aligned with values? Force a concrete, measurable answer along the **comparative dimensions whose canonical list lives in section 8**: the answer here determines the **qualifier** in the campaign thesis.
- USP probe: **does any element enjoy legal/patent exclusivity** (patent, monopoly, regulatory moat)? If yes, the USP route applies — the exclusivity itself is the differentiation; see the USP exception in section 1.

### Self-interrogation: 2 anchor pivots

When the copywriter is interrogating themselves (brand owner = copywriter), the 9 questions still apply, but two pivots help avoid self-deception:

- **Competition pivot**: instead of asking "what's unique about me?", ask "what's specifically wrong, slow, expensive, generic in what competitors do?". This usually reveals the UM more cleanly than direct self-praise.
- **Target pivot**: instead of asking "who do I serve?", ask "which specific sub-segment is my approach particularly well-suited to?". The UM crystallizes when it's anchored to a sub-segment rather than a generic audience.

### Quality gate

If after running the full 9-question interrogation:
- No question produces a substantive answer
- All "why" probes terminate in "we just do it that way"
- No comparison vs. competitors yields meaningful difference
- No specific target sub-segment alignment emerges

→ then a Type A (Existing) or Type B (Hidden) UM does NOT exist for this brand. The Strategist then either:

1. Proposes to construct a Type C (Transubstantiated) UM — see section 3, with all its warnings about the False Mechanism
2. Flags to the copywriter that this brand cannot credibly support a UM at the current sophistication stage; the funnel will need to compensate via stronger offer, identification copy, or other levers

## 5. False Mechanism — the trap

A False Mechanism is a mechanism with a name but no Reason Why. It looks like a UM but performs none of the persuasive work.

### Symptoms

- A proprietary-sounding name exists, but if asked "why does this make the product work better?", the brand owner gives a vague or circular answer
- The 9-question interrogation produces only generic responses
- The name was invented in a meeting without an underlying process or angle to support it
- The UM doesn't connect to a specific target sub-segment or a specific differentiation vs. competitors

### Diagnostic question

The single test: **"Can I write a paragraph that explains why this UM produces the result, citing concrete operational details, that would convince a skeptical reader?"**

If yes → real UM (or solid Type C with substantive backing). If no → False Mechanism.

### How to avoid

The 9-question interrogation in section 4, executed with depth and the discipline NOT to accept vague answers, is the prevention. The trap appears when the Strategist shortcuts the interrogation, accepts the brand owner's first answer, or constructs a name without going through the discovery work.

If the interrogation concludes that no real UM exists and the copywriter still wants to proceed, the Strategist flags this explicitly in the brief — the copy is then operating with a known weakness that must be compensated elsewhere.

### The False family — canonical list

The False Mechanism is one member of a family of six traps that share the same failure mode: **surface-level shape without underlying substance**. This is the canonical list — other files point here, never re-count.

1. **False Mechanism** — a mechanism with a name but no Reason Why behind it; looks like a UM but performs none of the persuasive work. (This file, §5.)
2. **False Proof** — a proof shape with no substance behind it: invented testimonials, fabricated stats, claimed certifications, inflated specificity. → [proof-elements](core/strategic-frameworks/proof-elements.md) §4
3. **False Name** — a name with the shape of a brand but none of the substance: a dressed-up generic descriptor, a hollow acronym, an aspirational tag the brand can't back up. → [naming](core/strategic-frameworks/naming.md) §8
4. **False Big Idea** — the shape of an angle of vision with none of the substance: reads as "creative" but converts as noise (hook-only, abstract, already-said, unsupportable, or product-disconnected). → [big-idea](core/strategic-frameworks/big-idea.md) §11
5. **False Offer** — the shape of an offer without the leverage: naked price, leftover bonuses, phantom scarcity, badge-only guarantee. → [offer-construction](core/strategic-frameworks/offer-construction.md) §10
6. **False Funnel** — the shape of an architecture without the movement: the prospect walks through but doesn't move (dead-weight touchpoints, doubled rings, state leaps). → [funnel-architecture](core/strategic-frameworks/funnel-architecture.md) §9

The defense in every case is the same: the relevant criteria checklist + stress tests, run rigorously and without shortcuts.

## 6. Choose your side — Original Problem vs New Better Solution

Once the UM is identified or constructed, the campaign argumentation can take one of two strategic shapes. The choice determines the entire structure of the copy at the Problem Aware level (see `awareness-levels.md`) and influences the chain of beliefs installed (see `chain-of-beliefs.md`).

**The choice is made by the copywriter.** The Strategist gathers the diagnostic answers, presents the analysis, may recommend, then asks. (Copywriter-led principle applies here as everywhere.)

### Route A — Original Problem (problem mechanism)

The argument: the prospect's current understanding of the cause of their problem is wrong or incomplete. A NEW root cause is installed; the brand's UM is the only one that addresses this root cause. All competitors are solving the wrong problem (the symptom, not the cause).

**Use when**: the brand's UM genuinely addresses a different cause than what the market believes, AND there's a real or articulable "root cause" angle that can be defended with proof.

**5 diagnostic questions (must have substantive answers to take Route A)**:

1. What is the real cause of the client's problem? (state the new mechanism)
2. What superficial symptoms are competitors addressing with their solutions?
3. What proof elements demonstrate the real cause to the client?
4. How does YOUR UM resolve the original (root) problem?
5. What are the benefits of resolving the original problem (vs. resolving symptoms)?

If all five questions have substantive answers → Route A is available and usually preferable (it installs a more durable conviction and a stronger differentiation moat).

### Route B — New Better Solution (solution mechanism)

The argument: the prospect's understanding of the problem is essentially correct. They've tried various solutions and been disappointed. The brand's UM offers a demonstrably superior solution to the SAME problem.

**Use when**: the brand competes head-to-head on the same problem the category addresses, with a measurably better approach. There is no different root cause to install — the work is on comparative superiority.

**4 diagnostic questions (must have substantive answers to take Route B)**:

1. In what way is your solution better than alternatives that address the same problem? (faster / safer / more durable / more comfortable / more aligned / cheaper over time)
2. WHY is your solution better? (the Reason Why + UM)
3. What proof elements support your UM's superiority?
4. What improvement benefits does your UM deliver vs. competitors' approaches?

### When in doubt

If both routes are technically available, choose **where you have more proof elements**. The route that can be more credibly defended in the body of the copy wins. Mixing routes within a single piece creates incoherence and breaks the reader's logical follow-through.

The copywriter selects. The Strategist documents the selection and rationale in the brief.

## 7. Articulating UMP and UMS

Once the type (section 3) is identified and the side (section 6) is chosen, the actual UMP and UMS content must be articulated. These are the two pieces of copy that get installed in the funnel — in the mechanism sections of VSL, advertorial, or landing page.

### UMP — Problem Mechanism articulation

The UMP is a 3-5 sentence block following this logical flow:

1. **State the prospect's current belief** about the cause of their problem (briefly).
2. **Reveal why that belief is incomplete or wrong** — the 1% of information they were missing.
3. **Introduce the real mechanism** — the actual cause they didn't know about.
4. **Explain why this mechanism produces the symptom** they feel daily.
5. **Bridge to the UMS**: "And [UMS / product name] is exactly what addresses this."

### UMS — Solution Mechanism articulation

The UMS is a 3-5 sentence block following this logical flow:

1. **Introduce the product / method** as the system that addresses the UMP.
2. **Explain the specific mechanism** within the product (the proprietary system, the ingredient, the process, the methodology).
3. **Show why this mechanism succeeds where alternatives failed** (they were addressing the wrong cause, or addressing the right cause inadequately).
4. **Anchor with proof** (case study, study, dramatic demonstration — full proof strategy lives in `proof-elements.md`).

### UMP/UMS weight by route

The "side" chosen in section 6 determines the relative weight of each part:

| Route | UMP weight | UMS weight |
|---|---|---|
| **A — Original Problem** | HEAVY (installs a NEW root cause; full 5-step articulation) | Strong (UMS shows how this addresses the new cause) |
| **B — New Better Solution** | LIGHT (confirms the prospect's existing cause-theory; brief acknowledgment) | DOMINANT (UMS is the center of gravity — comparative superiority) |

In **Route A**, the persuasive shift happens in the UMP — the new root cause is the conviction installed. The UMS then logically follows.

In **Route B**, the persuasive shift happens in the UMS — the prospect already agrees on the cause; the UMS shows why this approach is comparatively superior.

### Variant: UMS at Solution Aware (non-leader of category)

When the audience is Solution Aware AND the brand is NOT leader of category, the UMS needs an extra layer at the top: it must first establish that the CATEGORY itself works, before differentiating within it.

UMS extended structure for Solution Aware non-leader:

1. **Category layer** (1-2 sentences) — why this category of solution works as a broader approach
2. **Then the standard UMS** (3-5 sentences) — why YOUR product within this category is the right choice

The category layer is skipped when:
- The brand is leader of category (the category IS the brand's UMS)
- The audience is Problem Aware (they haven't accepted any category yet — the UMP work is dominant)
- The audience is Product Aware (they already accept the category — the UMS goes straight to product differentiation)

### The UMP/UMS bridge

Between the UMP and the UMS in the actual copy, there must be a logical bridge — a single transition sentence or short paragraph that makes the connection explicit:

> "Now that you understand [UMP], the question becomes: how do we address it? That's where [UMS / product] comes in."

This bridge is what turns two pieces into a logical pair. Without it, the prospect sees two disconnected sections — and the persuasive force of the UM collapses.

## 8. Building the campaign thesis

The campaign thesis is the **single conviction** the copy installs in the prospect's mind before they see the commercial offer. The whole funnel (Big Idea → Lead → Mechanism → Argumentation → Offer) is in service of installing this single conviction.

### The formula (canonical — 4 slots, identical wherever the thesis is written)

```
The [QUALIFIER] way for [AVATAR] to obtain [RESULT THE CLIENT WANTS]
is through [MY UNIQUE MECHANISM].
```

Four slots:

- **Qualifier** — the dominant comparative dimension on which the UM wins. Comes from question 9 of the interrogation (§4). **This section is the canonical home of the comparative dimensions** (question 9 and the cross-references point here, not to `mass-desire.md` — its "three dimensions" are the desire-scoring axes Urgency / Staying power / Scope, a different thing): fastest / safest / most effective / most comfortable / most reliable / most durable / most discreet / most personalized / most aligned with values / cheapest over time / most natural / most prestigious / only.
- **Avatar** — the target prospect the thesis speaks to. Comes from the avatar selected for this funnel (brief §3.5 Avatar reference block).
- **Result the client wants** — the specific outcome the avatar wants achieved. Comes from `mass-desire.md` (the selected desire) and the avatar's "Desires" / "Desired Situation" sections.
- **My Unique Mechanism** — the UM identified in this file. The name (if applicable) goes here.

### What the thesis is NOT

- **Not the offer.** The thesis is what makes the offer desirable, not the offer itself. The offer (price, bonus, urgency, guarantee) comes after.
- **Not the Big Idea.** The Big Idea is the angle of attack used to capture attention; the thesis is the conviction installed once attention is captured. Different roles in the funnel.
- **Not a tagline.** The thesis is not necessarily verbalized as-is in the headline. It's the internal conviction the whole argument is structured around. Sometimes the thesis IS the headline; sometimes the headline is more poetic and the thesis is the underlying logic.

### Position in the funnel

The thesis sits **between the mechanism explanation and the offer** in the funnel's logical structure:

```
Big Idea               (capture attention)
  ↓
Lead                   (amplify desire / install missing info)
  ↓
Mechanism explanation  (problem mechanism or solution mechanism)
  ↓
Thesis                 (the single conviction crystallized)
  ↓
Offer                  (presented to a now-convinced prospect)
  ↓
CTA
```

## 9. Naming the UM

### Where the naming work happens

The full naming framework lives in [naming](core/strategic-frameworks/naming.md). That file owns the brainstorming protocol, the 12 techniques, the criteria checklist, the stress tests, and the output format.

This section only carries the **UM-specific rules** that the Strategist (and the copywriter consulting `naming.md`) should keep in mind when the asset being named is specifically a Unique Mechanism.

### When the name doesn't exist yet

If the brand has no existing UM name AND the copywriter hasn't proposed one, the Strategist invokes `naming.md` to brainstorm candidates. The naming session returns a shortlist of 3-5 candidates with rationale; the copywriter selects from that shortlist (or rejects all and rebriefs).

If the brand already has a UM name OR the copywriter indicates one, skip the brainstorm and validate the existing name against the criteria in `naming.md` §5 and §6.3.

### When naming is required (by UM type)

The UM type (from §3) determines whether naming is mandatory:

- **Type C (Transubstantiated)**: naming is OBLIGATORY. The UM exists entirely in its name and the narrative around it. Without the name, there is no UM.
- **Type B (Hidden)**: naming is HIGHLY RECOMMENDED. The act of naming the previously unnamed IS the persuasive move.
- **Type A (Existing)**: naming is OPTIONAL. If the existing system is self-evident in its description, naming adds memorability but isn't structurally required. If the system is complex, naming consolidates it.

### How the name is built

All UM-specific naming craft — required register (technical / scientific / formal), Technique 11 (acronyms) as the default, Greek/Latin roots, per-letter defensibility of acronyms, the search-clean test — lives **only** in [naming](core/strategic-frameworks/naming.md) §6.3. When invoking `naming.md` for a UM, state that the asset is a UM: §6.3 governs the calibration.

## 10. Selection of the UM — operational sequence

The Strategist runs this during Phase 1 of brief building, AFTER `awareness-levels.md` has produced the awareness selection (which itself depends on `mass-desire.md`).

### Step 1 — Determine sophistication stage

Gather signals from:

- `brands/<brand>/research/competitor-research.md` — how many competitors? What's everyone saying? Are mechanism names already in the air?
- `brands/<brand>/research/market-awareness.md` — how saturated is the category?
- `brands/<brand>/research/unified-brief.md` — operational implications of the market state
- `brands/<brand>/competitors/*.md` — individual competitor messaging style

Present the diagnostic to the copywriter:
> "Signals suggest Stage [N]. At this stage, competitors typically [behavior]. The implication: [no UM needed | UM enters | UM elaborated | identification]."

Ask the copywriter to confirm or override.

### Step 2 — If Stage 1-2: no UM. Work on the promise.

Skip the rest of this file. Return to `mass-desire.md` to refine the promise. Move on to `chain-of-beliefs.md` and `proof-elements.md`.

### Step 3 — If Stage 3+: run the 9-question interrogation

Either:

- With the brand owner present: schedule the session(s), send questions in advance, conduct the interrogation with depth probing
- From existing brand materials only: extract answers from `brands/<brand>/brand.md`, `products.md`, `transcripts/`, and other materials; mark gaps where the answers are insufficient and surface them to the copywriter

### Step 4 — Identify the type of UM available

Based on the interrogation results:

- Substantive answers across most questions → **Existing UM (Type A)** is available
- Shared processes with competitors but unspoken in the market → **Hidden UM (Type B)** is available
- No substantive answers → only **Transubstantiated UM (Type C)** is available; flag the risk and the False Mechanism danger

### Step 5 — Present the findings to the copywriter

Show:

- Sophistication stage (confirmed in Step 1)
- Type of UM available (A, B, C)
- Top 1-2 candidate articulations of the UM (the actual content, not just the type)
- Recommended naming approach (and proposed names if useful)
- Recommended side (Original Problem vs New Better Solution) based on whether root-cause material emerged in the interrogation

### Step 6 — Ask the copywriter to select

The copywriter selects:

- The UM type (A, B, or C)
- The exact UM articulation approach
- The name (if naming is being used) — if no name exists yet in the brand AND the copywriter doesn't propose one, the Strategist invokes [naming](core/strategic-frameworks/naming.md) to run a brainstorm and returns with a shortlist of 3-5 candidates plus rationale; the copywriter then selects from that shortlist (or rejects all and rebriefs)
- The side (Original Problem or New Better Solution)
- The qualifier for the campaign thesis

The Strategist may recommend with rationale, but never closes the selection on its own.

### Step 7 — Articulate UMP and UMS

With type and side selected, the Strategist drafts the UMP and UMS as a coherent logical pair (templates and weight-by-route in section 7):

- **UMP** — the problem mechanism, 3-5 sentences
- **UMS** — the solution mechanism, 3-5 sentences
- **UMP/UMS bridge** — 1-2 sentence transition
- **Category layer** (only if Solution Aware non-leader) — 1-2 sentences before the UMS

The drafts are presented to the copywriter for review and refinement. The copywriter may rewrite, edit angles, or request alternatives. Once approved, both pieces enter the brief.

### Step 8 — Document in the brief

The brief contains an explicit UM block (see section 11).

## 11. Outputs into the funnel brief

For Stage 3+ funnels, the brief contains a Unique Mechanism block:

```
SOPHISTICATION
  Stage: [1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5]
  Rationale: [signals supporting this stage]

UNIQUE MECHANISM
  Type: [Existing | Hidden | Transubstantiated]
  Name (if used): "[name]"

  UMP (Problem Mechanism — 3-5 sentences):
    [the prospect's current belief about the cause]
    [why that belief is incomplete or wrong]
    [the real mechanism — the actual cause]
    [why this mechanism produces the symptom they feel]

  UMS (Solution Mechanism — 3-5 sentences):
    [the product / method as the system that addresses the UMP]
    [the specific mechanism within the product]
    [why this mechanism succeeds where alternatives failed]
    [proof anchor — case study / study / demonstration reference]

  UMP/UMS logical bridge:
    [1-2 sentence transition that makes the connection explicit]

  Category layer (only if Solution Aware non-leader):
    [1-2 sentences establishing why the category itself works, before the UMS]

  Reason Why (the substantive answer behind the UM):
    [why this makes the product work better for THIS prospect]

  Source signals: [files / interrogation outcomes that produced this]

SIDE
  Route: [Route A — Original Problem (heavy UMP) | Route B — New Better Solution (dominant UMS)]
  Rationale: [why this route over the other]

CAMPAIGN THESIS
  Filled formula: "The [qualifier] way for [avatar] to obtain [result] is through [UM]."

CROSS-REFERENCES
  Chain of beliefs installed: [reference to chain-of-beliefs.md — relevant rings of the awareness level]
  Proof elements required: [reference to proof-elements.md — the proof must support the UM]
```

For Stage 1-2 funnels:

```
SOPHISTICATION
  Stage: [1 | 2]
  Rationale: [signals supporting this stage]

UNIQUE MECHANISM
  Not used. At this stage, the work is on the promise itself.
  Promise approach: [Pure | Enlarged]
  Articulation of the promise: "[claim]"

CROSS-REFERENCES
  Chain of beliefs installed: [reference]
  Proof elements required: [reference]
```
